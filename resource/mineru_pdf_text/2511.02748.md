# Agentic World Modeling for 6G: Near-Real-Time Generative State-Space Reasoning

Farhad Rezazadeh, Member, IEEE, Amir Ashtari Gargari, Member, IEEE, Hatim Chergui, Senior Member, IEEE,

Sandra Lagen,´ Senior Member, IEEE, Merouane Debbah, Fellow, IEEE,

Houbing Song, Fellow, IEEE, and Lingjia Liu, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract—We argue that sixth-generation (6G) intelligence is not fluent token prediction but the capacity to imagine and choose—to simulate future scenarios, weigh trade-offs, and act with calibrated uncertainty. We reframe open radio access network (O-RAN) near-real-time (Near-RT) control via counterfactual dynamics and a world modeling (WM) paradigm that learns an action-conditioned generative state space. This enables quantitative “what-if” forecasting beyond large language models (LLMs) as the primary modeling primitive. Actions such as physical resource blocks (PRBs) are treated as first-class control inputs in a causal world model, and both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty are modeled for prediction and what-if analysis. An agentic, model predictive control (MPC)-based cross-entropy method (CEM) planner operates over short horizons, using prior-mean rollouts within data-driven PRB bounds to maximize a deterministic reward. The model couples multi-scale structured state-space mixtures (MS<sup>3</sup>M) with a compact stochastic latent to form WM– MS<sup>3</sup>M<sup>1</sup>, summarizing key performance indicators (KPIs) histories and predicting next-step KPIs under hypothetical PRB sequences. On realistic O-RAN traces, WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M cuts mean absolute error (MAE) by 1.69% versus MS<sup>3</sup>M with 32% fewer parameters and similar latency, and achieves 35–80% lower root mean squared error (RMSE) than attention/hybrid baselines with 2.3–4.1× faster inference, enabling rare-event simulation and offline policy screening.

Index Terms—6G, O-RAN, agentic, SSM, world mode

## I. INTRODUCTION

W <sup>ITHIN</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>emerging</sup> <sup>paradigm</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>6G</sup> <sup>agentic</sup> <sup>AI,</sup> <sup>world</sup> modeling [1]–[3] serves as the imagination engine that enables autonomous 6G agents to internally simulate, reason, and anticipate network management outcomes before acting. Unlike traditional reactive agents, those equipped with world models can construct latent representations of their 6G environment, perform what-if reasoning, and infer the causal consequences of potential actions, mirroring humanlike deliberation and foresight. This capability forms the foundation for goal-directed autonomy in complex, dynamic domains such as network automation, where agents must continuously interpret telemetry, predict system behavior, and plan interventions under uncertainty. In this context, world models go beyond digital twins, which primarily replicate network state through real-time data synchronization and visualization. While digital twins reflect the network’s current reality, world models learn its dynamics, allowing agents to imagine unobserved states, simulate hypothetical conditions, and optimize actions based on counterfactual reasoning. By integrating such imagination-driven reasoning, autonomous networks can progress toward TM Forum’s levels 4 and 5, where networks proactively plan and negotiate actions through predictive world understanding [4].

However, achieving such capabilities in O-RAN Near-RT control amounts to capturing, modeling, and reasoning over temporal dependencies under strict latency and safety constraints. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed for AI-native O-RAN [5]–[7], where they help with intent translation, policy/configuration generation, and naturallanguage interaction with operators. Yet, when LLMs are used as the primary modeling primitive for control, several limitations emerge: (i) token-centric training and weak inductive bias [8] for stochastic dynamics make it difficult to learn calibrated physical behavior from KPIs; (ii) exposure bias [9], [10] and off-policy extrapolation [11] degrade reliability when the model is rolled out for multi-step prediction or planning; (iii) many LLM-like or Transformer-based pipelines silently leak future information [12], [13], which inflates offline performance but does not translate into deployable Near-RT behavior; and (iv) uncertainty is typically not modeled in a control-grade way, making risk assessment and safety guards difficult to design and audit. These gaps suggest that LLMs are better suited as orchestration and explanation layers, while the core of Near-RT control should rely on leakage-safe, stateful, and uncertainty-aware dynamical models.

In this work, we propose an agentic world modeling perspective that places counterfactual dynamics [14], [15] at the center of control. Concretely, we treat physical resource blocks (PRBs) as first-class actions and learn a compact, causal, and probabilistic state-space model (SSM) [16] that supports (i) accurate next-step KPI prediction with calibrated uncertainty, (ii) imagination via action-conditioned rollouts (“what-if” forecasting), and (iii) short-horizon planning. We introduce WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M, which extends the strictly causal multiscale structured state-space mixtures (MS<sup>3</sup>M) from our previous work [17] by incorporating a lightweight stochastic latent variable and dual decoders. KPI windows—including PRB history—are summarized by a multi-scale SSM front end; a diagonal-Gaussian latent [18] captures residual uncertainty; and decoders produce both a full next-step frame and a heteroscedastic [19] target head. A model predictive control (MPC) planner [20] uses the cross-entropy method (CEM) [21], [22] in the model’s standardized space, operating within a datadriven PRB admissible set to avoid out-of-distribution actions. This design deliberately separates forecasting from decision making. The world model remains auditable and non-decisional, exposing predictive means, quantiles, and risk summaries, while the planner optimizes a short-horizon reward trading signal quality (signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR)/spectral efficiency (SE)/reference signal received power (RSRP) against block error rate (BLER)/Delay/PRB usage with a smoothness term. The same substrate supports imagination for proactive what-if analysis and policy evaluation without online trials.

## A. Related Work

1) LLM-based and Agentic O-RAN Analytics: Recent works explore LLM-centric and agentic paradigms for AI-native O-RAN. Intent-based management, xApp/rApp orchestration, and human-in-the-loop automation via LLMs have been proposed in [5]–[7], where LLMs translate operator intents into policies or configurations and help coordinate network functions. These approaches emphasize flexibility and ease of interaction but generally treat the underlying network behavior as a black box and focus on high-level control or policy templates. They do not provide a calibrated, action-conditioned dynamical model of KPIs, nor do they explicitly support quantitative counterfactual analysis under resource constraints.

2) Efficient Sequence Models and Time-Series Foundations: A large body of work has improved sequence modeling efficiency and expressiveness for long horizons. RWKV [23] blends RNN-style recurrence with attention-like mixing for streaming inference, but remains sensitive to exposure bias under one-step-ahead evaluation. Performers [24] replace softmax attention with kernelized approximations, achieving lineartime attention suitable for long contexts, while RetNet [25] introduces retentive state-space updates with O(T ) complexity and strong long-memory capabilities. Time-series foundation models such as Chronos-GPT and Chronos-T5 [26] tokenize numerical sequences and leverage language-modeling priors for forecasting, with Chronos-GPT using a causal decoder and Chronos-T5 using a denoising seq2seq architecture with bidirectional context. These models provide powerful priors for generic sequence prediction, but: (i) they are typically trained and evaluated with token-centric losses that are not unit-aware; (ii) many use bidirectional context or sequence-level normalization that would amount to data leakage in Near-RT control; and (iii) they rarely treat control variables (e.g., PRBs) as explicit actions in a causal dynamical system.

3) State-Space Models and World Models for Control: Modern SSMs such as S4 and variants [27] provide structured, long-memory filters with stable dynamics and lineartime convolutions. These architectures have been successfully applied to generic sequence tasks and time series, and they naturally align with the controlled dynamical-systems view of wireless networks. In parallel, world models and model-based RL (e.g., [1], [28]–[30]) show that learned latent dynamics can support imagination, planning, and sample-efficient decision making via MPC or policy optimization over simulated rollouts. However, most existing applications of SSMs to networking focus on observational forecasting (e.g., traffic/load prediction) without explicit control channels or counterfactual planning, and most world-modeling work targets robotics or games with different observables and constraints than O-RAN telemetry. Recent work on counterfactual or causal world models [14], [15] moves closer to our goal but does not address Near-RT RIC integration, PRB-constrained planning, or leakage-safe telemetry pipelines.

## B. Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions, each directly tied to the gaps above:

• Paradigm: Agentic world modeling for 6G (prediction/imagination → choice). We formalize a world-model first contract for O-RAN in which a calibrated, actionconditioned model provides both factual prediction and counterfactual imagination, and an outer-loop planner (MPC/CEM) chooses actions. Gap addressed: Unlike LLMcentric or rule-based approaches [5]–[7], which entangle modeling and policy in token space, our design makes PRBs explicit actions and separates prediction from decision, improving safety, auditability, and robustness to data shifts.

• Architecture: WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M (multi-scale SSM + compact latent + dual decoders). We extend the strictly causal MS<sup>3</sup>M backbone [17] with a lightweight stochastic latent and two coordinated decoders (a full-frame head and a heteroscedastic target head with a bounded AR skip). Gap addressed: Existing SSM-based or foundation time series models [26], [27] either ignore uncertainty or treat control variables as passive features. Our architecture provides calibrated epistemic/aleatoric uncertainty [19] and explicitly conditions on PRBs, enabling control-grade forecasting and what-if rollouts.

• Leakage-safe, unit-aware learning pipeline for O-RAN telemetry. We enforce chronological splits, train-only scalers, KL annealing [31], posterior/prior mixing, and light robustness augmentations (feature-channel dropout, small input noise). Gap addressed: Many sequence models implicitly leak future information via bidirectional context or global normalization, which is incompatible with Near-RT deployment. Our pipeline ensures that offline scores reflect true on-policy behavior with KPIs reported in physical units.

• Quantified uncertainty and action-conditioned what-if forecasting. Test-time prior sampling yields means, intervals, and risk summaries for next-step and short-horizon KPIs under candidate PRB sequences constrained to data-driven admissible ranges. Gap addressed: Traditional predictors provide point estimates only, and black-box policies cannot explain risk. WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M enables questions like “If we ±20% PRBs, what is the BLER exceedance risk in the next H steps?”, supporting risk-aware control and offline policy screening.

• Short-horizon planning via MPC/CEM in standardized space. We instantiate a Near-RT-compatible planner that optimizes a shaped reward trading SINR/SE/RSRP against BLER/Delay and PRB cost with a smoothness penalty, all in standardized space, and maps actions back to physical units. Gap addressed: Prior O-RAN control strategies are often heuristic and myopic, while generic RL can be sampleinefficient or unsafe. Our MPC/CEM planner is sampleefficient, auditable, and constrained to in-distribution PRB ranges derived from training data.

• Accuracy–efficiency under Near-RT constraints. On realistic O-RAN traces, WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M improves MAE by 1.69 % over an equally causal MS<sup>3</sup>M while using ≈ 32 % fewer parameters and similar latency, and outperforms attention/hybrid baselines by 35–80% RMSE with 2.3–4.1× faster inference. Gap addressed: This demonstrates that a world model with SSM inductive bias and compact latent can deliver control-grade accuracy at edge-friendly cost, addressing the practical deployability gap of many Transformer-family models.

• Reproducible artifacts and O-RAN integration. We release code for WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M, the leakage-safe training/inference pipeline, and MPC/CEM planning (non-commercial license), plus curated O-RAN KPI resources, and we explicitly map the world model and planner into the Near-RT RIC/xApp workflow. Gap addressed: This bridges the frequent disconnect between algorithmic proposals and O-RAN-compliant implementations, enabling follow-on work on agentic world models in 6G networks.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II details the system model and O-RAN integration. Section III presents the WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M architecture and the leakage-safe training/inference pipeline. Section V reports quantitative results, uncertainty diagnostics, what-if forecasting, and MPC/CEM planning. We conclude in Section VI.

## II. SYSTEM MODEL

We adopt the O-RAN architecture in Figure 1, where an agentic controller is realized as the composition of a learned world model xApp and an outer-loop MPC/CEM planner. Both run in the Near-RT RIC and interact with the E2 node [32], [33], the Non-RT RIC/service management and orchestration (SMO), and an offline data/machine learning operations (MLOps) pipeline. In this setting, the E2 node (i.e., O-RAN distributed unit (O-DU) and O-RAN central unit (O-CU)) executes scheduling and lower-layer procedures. Every aggregation period $\Delta$ (typically on the order of 10–1000 ms), it sends aggregated KPI history for the last interval (KPI history (last ∆) in Figure 1) to the Near-RT RIC over the E2 interface. It applies the selected PRB action produced by the planner (Selected PRB action (per ∆)). At the same time, the E2 node produces KPI traces that are exported to the offline data/MLOps pipeline (KPI traces (for offline training)) and later used to retrain or fine-tune the world model. In a live deployment, these KPIs would be delivered as aggregated telemetry over the E2 interface from the O-DU and O-CU. In our experiments, we instead use an offline dataset of such KPIs collected from an O-RAN test setup, as described in Section IV-A.

The Near-RT RIC hosts both the $\mathrm { W M - M S ^ { 3 } M }$ world model xApp and the MPC/CEM planner. At each decision time t, the Near-RT RIC receives a window of aggregated KPIs and PRB usage from the E2 node and assembles them into a causal input window. The planner proposes candidate PRB sequences (the Candidate PRB sequences block in Figure 1) and sends them, together with the KPI window, to the world model. The world model evaluates each candidate by rolling out short-horizon, action-conditioned KPI trajectories and returning “Forecasts for evaluation” and “What-if forecasts (KPI trajectories)”. The planner then scores these trajectories according to an operatorspecified reward and selects the next PRB action, which is sent back to the E2 node as the “Selected PRB action (per ∆)”.

The Non-RT RIC/SMO supervises this Near-RT loop at a slower timescale. It configures high-level policies, reward weights, and constraints that define the Near-RT objective for the planner, as indicated by the arrow “Policies, reward weights, constraints” feeding into the MPC/CEM block in Figure 1. It also distributes model configurations and checkpoints to the world model xApp (“Model config/checkpoints” and “Model updates/checkpoints”), typically after offline retraining on the KPI traces produced by the E2 node and processed in the offline data/MLOps pipeline. In the other direction, the Near-RT RIC reports KPI logs and performance summaries to the Non-RT RIC/SMO for monitoring and longer-term optimization.

Let $\mathbf { x } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { F }$ denote the KPI vector aggregated over [(t − $1 ) \Delta , t \Delta )$ for a given sector (see Table I for major notations). It includes radio-quality and traffic/load indicators (e.g., RSRP, SINR, CQI, SE, BLER, delay, PRB usage). The Near-RT controller acts through a scalar control $u _ { t } \equiv \mathrm { P R B } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } .$ representing the scheduled PRBs in period t. We model the radio environment as a controlled, partially observed stochastic process. A latent state $x _ { t }$ summarizes unobserved propagation, interference, and traffic conditions. The dynamics follow a generic state-space form

$$
x _ {t + 1} = f _ {\theta} (x _ {t}, u _ {t}) + w _ {t}, \qquad \mathbf {x} _ {t} = h _ {\theta} (x _ {t}) + v _ {t},\tag{1}
$$

where $w _ { t }$ and $v _ { t }$ capture stochastic variability and unmodeled effects. We do not attempt to reconstruct $x _ { t }$ explicitly. Instead, all predictions and planning are based on a causal summary of the observable history, which is exactly what is available to an xApp at the Near-RT RIC. Let $\mathcal { F } _ { t }$ denote the σ-algebra generated by $\{ \mathbf { x } _ { \tau } , u _ { \tau } \} _ { \tau \leq t }$ . At time $t ,$ the Near-RT RIC has access to a standardized lookback window of length $L ,$

$$
\mathbf {X} _ {t} = (\mathbf {x} _ {t - L + 1}, \ldots , \mathbf {x} _ {t}) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {L \times F}, \qquad u _ {t - L + 1: t} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {L \times 1},\tag{2}
$$

and the one-step-ahead target $\mathbf { y } _ { t + 1 } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { O } , \quad O \in \{ 1 , F \}$ where $\mathbf { y } _ { t + 1 }$ may represent a single KPI (e.g., RSRP) or the full KPI vector. By construction, $\left( { \mathbf { X } } _ { t } , { u } _ { t - L + 1 : t } \right)$ is $\mathcal { F } _ { t }$ -measurable and $\mathbf { y } _ { t + 1 } \ \mathrm { i s } \ \mathcal { F } _ { t + 1 }$ -measurable. Any predictor of the form $f _ { \theta }$ $( \mathbf { X } _ { t } , u _ { t - L + 1 : t } ) \mapsto { \widehat { \mathbf { y } } } _ { t + 1 }$ is thus strictly causal and deployable in <sup>b</sup>the Near-RT control loop. To keep planning within the region supported by the data and by scheduler limits, we restrict actions to a data-driven admissible set $\mathcal { U } \subset \mathbb { R }$ . ## III. WORLD-MODELED MULTI-SCALE STRUCTURED STATE-SPACE MIXTURE

We introduce $W M { \mathrm { - } } M S ^ { 3 } M ,$ , a strictly causal forecaster that augments multi-scale structured state-space mixtures with a small latent world model. Each layer applies depthwise causal filters derived from HiPPO–LegS [27] dynamics at multiple time scales and then mixes channels via squeeze–excitation [34] gating and a gated linear unit (GLU) [35]. A compact latent z is inferred from history (prior) or history-plus-future (posterior, training-only) and conditions two decoders: a full next-step mean squared error (MSE) reconstruction head (fixedvariance surrogate) and a heteroscedastic target head with an autoregressive (AR) [36] skip. Training is leakage-safe (trainonly scalers, chronological split) and uses KL annealing [31] and scheduled posterior mixing. Monte Carlo (MC) [37] prior sampling averages target-head means at test time; uncertainty is available via a variance decomposition. Algorithms 1 and 2 summarize the procedures. For action-conditioned decision support, we perform short-horizon planning via MPC/CEM with deterministic prior-mean rollouts; see Algorithm 3.

## A. Problem Setup and Causality

Let $\{ { \bf x } _ { t } \} _ { t = 1 } ^ { T }$ be a multivariate KPI time series with $\mathbf { x } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { F }$ Fix a lookback horizon L and construct input–target pairs

$$
\mathbf {X} _ {t} = (\mathbf {x} _ {t - L + 1}, \ldots , \mathbf {x} _ {t}) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {L \times F}, \qquad \mathbf {y} _ {t + 1} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {O},\tag{4}
$$

where $O \in \{ 1 , F \}$ denotes, respectively, a univariate target (e.g., one KPI) or a multivariate target (all F KPIs) [38], [39].

On the training split, we fit per-feature standardization statistics and reuse them across all splits:

$$
\widetilde {\mathbf {X}} = (\mathbf {X} - \boldsymbol {\mu} _ {x}) \oslash \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {x}, \qquad \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} (\mathbf {y} _ {i ^ {\star}} - \mu_ {y}) / \sigma_ {y}, & O = 1, \\ (\mathbf {y} - \boldsymbol {\mu} _ {y}) \oslash \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {y}, & O = F. \end{array} \right.\tag{5}
$$

Since $\mathbf { X } _ { t }$ is $\mathcal { F } _ { t }$ -measurable (depends only on observations up to time t) and $\mathbf { y } _ { t + 1 }$ lies one step ahead, any measurable predictor $f _ { \theta } ( \widetilde { \mathbf { X } } _ { t } )$ is causal. (Alg. 1, line 1; Alg. 2, line 1).

2) Bilinear (Tustin) Discretization: For $\Delta t > 0$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {A (\Delta t) = \left(I - \frac {\Delta t}{2} A _ {\mathrm{ct}}\right) ^ {- 1} \left(I + \frac {\Delta t}{2} A _ {\mathrm{ct}}\right),} \\ & {k _ {c} [ 0 ] = C _ {c} B _ {c} + D _ {c}, \qquad k _ {c} [ \tau ] = C _ {c} A (\Delta t) ^ {\tau} B _ {c},} \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

where $c \in \{ 1 , \ldots , d \}$ indexes channels. A matrix $A _ { \mathrm { { c t } } }$ is called Hurwitz if all its eigenvalues have strictly negative real parts, which ensures asymptotic stability of the continuous-time LTI system ${ \dot { x } } = A _ { \mathrm { c t } } x$ [40]. A matrix A is called Schur-stable if all its eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit disk, $\mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , | \lambda _ { i } ( A ) | < 1$ which is the standard stability notion for discrete-time systems $x _ { k + 1 } = A x _ { k }$ [40]. For the bilinear (Tustin) discretization in (7), standard results on Pade/bilinear transforms imply that if´ $A _ { \mathrm { { c t } } }$ is Hurwi $^ { \mathrm { t } , }$ then $A ( \Delta t )$ is Schur-stable for any $\Delta t > 0 [ 4 1 ]$ [42], so the tap energy decays geometrically and a finite kernel length $L _ { k }$ is a controlled truncation.

3) Depthwise Causal Convolution and Mixture: Let $\widetilde { \mathbf { X } } \mapsto$ $H ^ { ( 0 ) } = \widetilde { \mathbf { X } } W _ { \mathrm { i n } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { L \times d }$ . At layer $\ell ,$ we form M components with learned $\Delta t ^ { ( \ell , m ) } > 0$ and taps $k _ { c } ^ { ( \ell , m ) } [ \cdot ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { L _ { k } }$ and then sum them

$$
k _ {c} ^ {(\ell)} [ \cdot ] = \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} k _ {c} ^ {(\ell , m)} [ \cdot ], \qquad U _ {t, c} ^ {(\ell)} = \sum_ {\tau = 0} ^ {L _ {k} - 1} k _ {c} ^ {(\ell)} [ \tau ] H _ {t - \tau , c} ^ {(\ell - 1)},\tag{8}
$$

where $c \in \{ 1 , \ldots , d \}$ indexes channels and we define $H _ { s , c } ^ { ( \ell - 1 ) } ~ = ~ 0$ for all $s \ < \ 1$ . In other words, we pad the sequence with $( L _ { k } - 1 )$ zeros on the $l e f t$ (pre-padding) and no padding on the right, so that $U _ { t , c } ^ { ( \check { \ell } ) }$ depends only on $\{ H _ { s , c } ^ { ( \ell - 1 ) } : 1 \le s \le t \}$ and never on future positions. We set $L _ { k } { = } L$ in code and implement this as a depthwise 1D convolution with ${ \mathfrak { g r o u p s } } = d$ and left zero-padding, matching the theoretical per-channel SSM. (Alg. 1, line 3 and lines 4–6).

4) Positivity Map for ∆t: We parameterize $\Delta t$ as $\Delta t = \phi ( \tau )$ with

$$
\phi (\tau) = \log \big (1 + \exp (\tau) \big) + \epsilon , \qquad \epsilon > 0,\tag{9}
$$

so each learned time scale is strictly positive. The function $\phi ( \tau )$ is the standard softplus nonlinearity (a smooth approximation of $\operatorname* { m a x } \{ 0 , \tau \} )$ , which maps $\mathbb { R } \to ( 0 , \infty )$ and has well-behaved derivatives for both negative and positive inputs. This choice ensures that all $\Delta t$ used in the bilinear (Tustin) discretization remain strictly positive (via the small offset ϵ), avoiding invalid or numerically degenerate step sizes while allowing unconstrained optimization in the parameter τ .

## E. Mathematical Details of the Latent World Model

1) Variables and Conditionings: At each time $t ,$ the standardized lookback window $\widetilde { \mathbf { X } } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { L \times F }$ is mapped by the SSM stack into a deterministic summary $d _ { t } = f _ { \theta } ( \widetilde { \mathbf { X } } _ { t } ) \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d }$ (the last-step embedding). The (train-only) standardized next-step vector is $\widetilde { \mathbf { y } } _ { \mathrm { f u l l } , t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \bar { F } }$ , and the (possibly univariate) standardized target is $\widetilde { \mathbf { y } } _ { t } ^ { ( O ) } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { O }$ with $O \in \{ 1 , F \}$ . A compact latent state $\boldsymbol { z } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ captures information not determined by $\mathbf { } d _ { t }$ alone.

2) Generative Parameterization (Conditional VAE View): We specify a diagonal-Gaussian prior and two conditional decoders

$$
p _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}) = \mathcal {N} \big (\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {p} (\boldsymbol {d} _ {t}), \mathrm{diag} (\boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {p} ^ {2} (\boldsymbol {d} _ {t})) \big),\tag{14}
$$

$$
p _ {\theta} (\tilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text {full}, t} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {z} _ {t}) \propto \exp \Big (- \frac {1}{2 \sigma_ {\text {fixed}} ^ {2}} \big \| \tilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text {full}, t} - \boldsymbol {\mu} _ {\text {full}} ([ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}; \boldsymbol {z} _ {t} ]) \big \| _ {2} ^ {2} \Big),\tag{15}
$$

$$
p _ {\theta} \left(\tilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {t} ^ {(O)} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {z} _ {t}, \tilde {\mathbf {x}} _ {t}\right) = \mathcal {N} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {t} \left(\left[ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}; \boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \right]\right) + \alpha \boldsymbol {b} _ {t}, \operatorname{diag} \left(\sigma_ {t} ^ {2} \left(\left[ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}; \boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \right]\right)\right)\right),\tag{16}
$$

where ${ \pmb b } _ { t } = f _ { \mathrm { a r } } ( \widetilde { \mathbf { x } } _ { t } )$ is a small MLP of the last standardized input vector and $\alpha = \operatorname { t a n h } ( \kappa ) \in ( - 1 , 1 )$ is a learned bounded $\mathrm { g a i n } . ^ { 2 }$ Equation (15) is implemented as MSE (i.e., a fixedvariance Gaussian surrogate) for the $f u l l$ reconstruction head, and Equation (16) for the target head.

3) Inference Model and Leakage $S a f e t y .$ : Training uses an amortized Gaussian posterior

$$
q _ {\theta} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}, \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text { full }, t}\right) = \mathcal {N} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {q} \left(\left[ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}; \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text { full }, t} \right]\right), \operatorname{diag} \left(\boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {q} ^ {2} \left(\left[ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}; \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text { full }, t} \right]\right)\right)\right),\tag{17}
$$

in the standard variational autoencoder style with amortized inference [43]. The standardization parameters $( \mu _ { x } , \sigma _ { x } )$ and $( \mu _ { y } , \sigma _ { y } ) \mathrm { o r } ( \pmb { \mu } _ { y } , \pmb { \sigma } _ { y } )$ are estimated on the training split only and then kept fixed for validation/test, to avoid look-ahead leakage in the time-series setting [44]. For numerical stability in the heteroscedastic target head, we clip log $\sigma _ { t } ^ { 2 }$ to a bounded interval $( { \bf e } . { \bf g } . , ~ [ - 8 , 8 ] )$ before computing the likelihood, following common practice in deep uncertainty-aware regression [19].

4) KL-annealed ELBO with Scheduled Posterior Mixing: Introduce a Bernoulli switch $s _ { t } \sim \mathrm { B e r n o u l l i } ( \pi _ { e } )$ sampled per mini-batch. Define the chosen encoder

$$
\tilde {q} _ {t} (\boldsymbol {z} _ {t}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} q _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}, \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text { full }, t}), & s _ {t} = 1 \quad \text {(posterior phase)}, \\ p _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}), & s _ {t} = 0 \quad \text {(prior phase)}, \end{array} \right.\tag{18}
$$

so that, when $s _ { t } = 0$ (prior phase), we set $\tilde { q } _ { t } \equiv p _ { \theta } ( z _ { t } \mid d _ { t } )$ and the KL term evaluates to zero (KL between identical Gaussians). With $\beta _ { e }$ the KL weight at epoch e, the per-sample ELBO reads

$$
\begin{array}{l}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO},t} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\sim\tilde{q}_{t}}\big[\log p_{\theta}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{full},t}|\boldsymbol{d}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\big]}_{\text{full reconstruction (MSE surrogate)}}\\ +\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\sim\tilde{q}_{t}}\big[\log p_{\theta}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{(O)}|\boldsymbol{d}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\big]}_{\substack{\text{target}\\ \text{prediction}}}\\ -  \beta_{e}\underbrace{\text{KL}\big(\tilde{q}_{t}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})\parallel p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{d}_{t})\big)}_{\text{latent regularization}}. \end{array}\tag{19}
$$

The training loss in Equation (32) is a weighted negative ELBO surrogate: $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { r e c o n } }$ approximates the first term (fixedvariance Gaussian via MSE); ${ \mathcal { L } } _ { \mathrm { t } }$ and $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { h u b } }$ the second; $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { k l } }$ the third; and ${ \mathcal { L } } _ { \mathrm { c o n s } }$ encourages agreement between the two decoders on the target coordinate. Schedules $\beta _ { e }$ and $\pi _ { e }$ follow the linear ramps defined in §III-F,

$$
\mathrm{KL} \big (\mathcal {N} _ {q} \| \mathcal {N} _ {p} \big) = \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {d _ {z}} \left[ \frac {\sigma_ {q , j} ^ {2}}{\sigma_ {p , j} ^ {2}} + \frac {(\mu_ {p , j} - \mu_ {q , j}) ^ {2}}{\sigma_ {p , j} ^ {2}} - 1 + \log \frac {\sigma_ {p , j} ^ {2}}{\sigma_ {q , j} ^ {2}} \right].\tag{20}
$$

5) Reparameterization and Gradients: For either $\tilde { q } _ { t }$ , sample with the reparameterization technique:

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {t} = \boldsymbol {\mu} _ {\tilde {q} _ {t}} + \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {\tilde {q} _ {t}} \odot \boldsymbol {\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol {\epsilon} \sim \mathcal {N} (\boldsymbol {0}, I),\tag{21}
$$

which yields unbiased, low-variance Monte Carlo estimates of the expectations in Equation (19).

6) Predictive Distribution and MC Calibration: At test time, the prior-only predictive for the standardized target is

$$
p _ {\theta} (\widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {t} ^ {(O)} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}, \widetilde {\mathbf {x}} _ {t}) = \int p _ {\theta} (\widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {t} ^ {(O)} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}, \boldsymbol {z} _ {t}, \widetilde {\mathbf {x}} _ {t}) p _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \mid \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}) d \boldsymbol {z} _ {t}.\tag{22}
$$

We approximate Equation (22) by MC, draw $\boldsymbol { z } _ { t } ^ { ( s ) } \sim p _ { \theta } \big ( \boldsymbol { z } _ { t } \mid d _ { t } \big )$ $s { = } 1 { : } S$ , and set

$$
\begin{array}{c} \overline {{\mathbf {y}}} _ {\mathrm{t}} = \frac {1}{S} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \left(\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {t} ([ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}; \boldsymbol {z} _ {t} ^ {(s)} ]) + \alpha   \boldsymbol {b} _ {t}\right), \\ \widehat {\mathbf {y}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \mu_ {y} + \sigma_ {y}   \overline {{\mathbf {y}}} _ {\mathrm{t}}, & O = 1, \\ \boldsymbol {\mu} _ {y} + \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {y} \odot \overline {{\mathbf {y}}} _ {\mathrm{t}}, & O = F. \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{23}
$$

When the decoder in Equation (16) is heteroscedastic, an unbiased estimator of the standardized predictive variance decomposes as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \widehat {\mathrm{Var}} [ \widetilde {\boldsymbol {y}} _ {t} ^ {(O)} ] = \underbrace {\frac {1}{S} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \sigma_ {t} ^ {2} ([ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t} ; \boldsymbol {z} _ {t} ^ {(s)} ])} _ {\text {aleatoric}} \\ \qquad + \underbrace {\frac {1}{S - 1} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \Big (\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {t} ([ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t} ; \boldsymbol {z} _ {t} ^ {(s)} ]) - \overline {{\boldsymbol {\mu}}} _ {t} \Big) ^ {2}} _ {\text {epistemic (MC)}} \\ \overline {{\boldsymbol {\mu}}} _ {t} = \frac {1}{S} \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {S} \boldsymbol {\mu} _ {t} ([ \boldsymbol {d} _ {t}; \boldsymbol {z} _ {t} ^ {(s)} ]), \end{array}\tag{24}
$$

with the AR skip $\chi \smash { b _ { t } }$ deterministic given $\widetilde { \mathbf { X } } _ { t }$ . Variance in original units follows by multiplying elementwise with $\sigma _ { y } ^ { 2 }$ (or $\pmb { \sigma } _ { y } ^ { 2 } )$

7) Consistency Between Decoders: Let $i ^ { \star }$ denote the target index when O=1. The consistency loss ${ \mathcal { L } } _ { \mathrm { c o n s } }$ penalizes the discrepancy between the target-head mean $\widehat { \mathbf { y } } _ { \mathrm { t } , t } = \pmb { \mu } _ { t } + \alpha \pmb { b } _ { t }$ and the corresponding coordinate of the full-head output, i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { \left. \widehat { \mathbf { y } } _ { \mathrm { t } , t } - \widehat { \mathbf { y } } _ { \mathrm { f u l l } , t } [ i ^ { \star } ] \right. _ { 2 } ^ { \sum } , } \end{array}$ encouraging the two conditionals to agree on the target coordinate while allowing the full head to model residual cross-feature structure useful for representation learning.

8) AR Skip as a Small Learned Control: Writing the target likelihood as $\widetilde { \bf y } _ { t } ^ { ( O ) } = \pmb { \mu } _ { t } ( [ { \pmb d } _ { t } ; { \pmb z } _ { t } ] ) + \alpha f _ { \mathrm { a r } } ( \widetilde { \bf x } _ { t } ) + \epsilon _ { t } , \quad \epsilon _ { t } \sim$ $\mathcal { N } ( 0 , \mathrm { d i a g } ( \pmb { \sigma } _ { t } ^ { 2 } ) )$ , the term α $f _ { \mathrm { a r } } ( \widetilde { \mathbf { x } } _ { t } )$ (with $\alpha = \operatorname { t a n h } ( \kappa ) \in$ $( - 1 , 1 ) )$ acts as a known exogenous control computed from the most recent inputs by a small MLP, which improves shorthorizon fidelity without violating causality. In practice, we clamp log $\sigma _ { t } ^ { 2 } ~ { \sf t o } ~ [ - 8 , 8 ]$ to avoid numerical pathologies.

The latent is identifiable up to affine transformations that can be absorbed by the decoders; the prior/posterior are diagonal to avoid degenerate rotations. Scale symmetries across $\left( d _ { t } , z _ { t } \right)$ are partially fixed by LayerNorm/squeeze–excitation and by the KL penalty Equation (20). Equations (14)–(19) formalize WM– MS<sup>3</sup>M as a causal, conditional VAE whose encoder switches between posterior and prior according to a scheduled Bernoulli, enabling stable training (via KL annealing) and calibrated mean inference (via prior MC in Equations (22)–(24)) without label leakage.

## F. Leakage-Safe Objectives and Schedules

For a mini-batch $B ,$ we combine five terms (all in standard ized space):

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{recon}} = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {B} |} \sum_ {t \in \mathcal {B}} \| \widehat {\mathbf {y}} _ {\mathrm{full}, t} - \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\mathrm{full}, t} \| _ {2} ^ {2},\tag{25}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{t}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1}{| \mathcal {B} |} \sum_ {t} \frac {1}{2} \bigg (\log \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {t} ^ {2} + \frac {(\widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {t} ^ {(O)} - \widehat {\mathbf {y}} _ {\mathrm{t} , t}) ^ {2}}{\exp (\log \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {t} ^ {2})} + \log 2 \pi \bigg), & \text {hetero}, \\ \frac {1}{| \mathcal {B} |} \sum_ {t} \| \widehat {\mathbf {y}} _ {\mathrm{t}, t} - \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {t} ^ {(O)} \| _ {2} ^ {2}, & \text {homo}, \end{array} \right.\tag{26}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{hub}} = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {B} |} \sum_ {t} \mathrm{Huber} _ {\delta} \left(\widehat {\mathbf {y}} _ {t, t} - \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {t} ^ {(O)}\right),\tag{27}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{cons}} = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {B} |} \sum_ {t} \left\| \widehat {\mathbf {y}} _ {\mathrm{t}, t} - \widehat {\mathbf {y}} _ {\mathrm{full}, t} [ i ^ {\star} ] \right\| _ {2} ^ {2},\tag{28}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{kl}} = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {B} |} \sum_ {t} \mathrm{KL} \big (\mathcal {N} (\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {q}, \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {q} ^ {2}) \left\| \mathcal {N} (\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {p}, \boldsymbol {\sigma} _ {p} ^ {2})\right),\tag{29}
$$

where $\widetilde { \mathbf { y } } _ { \mathrm { f u l l } }$ is the standardized next-step F -vector and $i ^ { \star }$ selects the target component when $O { = } 1$ . For the heteroscedastic case lets the model predict a separate variance $\sigma _ { t } ^ { 2 }$ for each target (data-dependent noise level), while the homoscedastic case corresponds to a standard fixed-variance Gaussian (or equivalently, an MSE loss) with constant noise across all samples [19]. The aligned rollout penalty averages teacherforced multi-step reconstruction errors when aligned labels are provided:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\text { roll }} = \frac {1}{K} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left\| \widehat {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text { full }} ^ {(k)} - \widetilde {\mathbf {y}} _ {\text { full }} \right\| _ {2} ^ {2}.\tag{30}
$$

We use a linear schedule for the KL weight and for the posterior-use probability over training epochs e:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\beta_ {e} = \beta_ {\mathrm{start}} + (\beta_ {\mathrm{end}} - \beta_ {\mathrm{start}}) \min \bigg (1, \frac {e}{E _ {\mathrm{kl}}} \bigg),} \\ & {\pi_ {e} = \pi_ {0} + (\pi_ {1} - \pi_ {0}) \min \bigg (1, \frac {e}{E _ {\mathrm{kl}}} \bigg).} \end{array}\tag{31}
$$

Here $\beta _ { e }$ is the epoch-dependent weight applied to the KL regularizer $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { k l } }$ in Equation (32), and $\pi _ { e }$ is the epoch-dependent probability of using the posterior encoder (instead of the prior) when sampling the latent $\mathbf { z } _ { t }$ during training. The overall objective is

$$
\begin{array}{r} \mathcal {L} = \alpha_ {\mathrm{rec}} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{recon}} + \alpha_ {\mathrm{t}} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{t}} + \alpha_ {\mathrm{hub}} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{hub}} \\ + \alpha_ {\mathrm{cons}} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{cons}} + \beta_ {e} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{kl}} + \alpha_ {\mathrm{roll}} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{roll}}. \end{array}\tag{32}
$$

1) Optimization and Early Stopping: We use mixedprecision training, AdamW with weight decay λ, Cosine Warm Restarts, gradient-norm clipping $\| g \| \leq c _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } .$ , and patiencebased early stopping on validation loss. The cosine scheduler is stepped per mini-batch using a fractional epoch counter (rather than once per epoch) to match the implementation. All scalers are fit on train only; metrics (MSE, RMSE, and MAE) are reported after inverse standardization. (Alg. 1, lines 13–24; line 24 returns).

## G. Prior-Sampling Inference (MC Averaging)

At test time, compute d, form the prior $( \mu _ { p } , \sigma _ { p } ^ { 2 } )$ as in Equation (14), draw $\boldsymbol { z } ^ { ( s ) } \sim \mathcal { N } ( \pmb { \mu } _ { p } , \mathrm { d i a g } ( \pmb { \sigma } _ { p } ^ { 2 } ) )$ , decode $\mu _ { t } ^ { ( s ) }$ add the AR skip, and average. $$

For uncertainty, use the variance decomposition in Equation (24).

## IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

## A. Data Collection

The data-collection steps and all related implementation details are discussed comprehensively in our previous works [17], [45], [46]. The dataset<sup>3</sup> is publicly available to support reproducibility and enable follow-on research.

## B. KPIs and Preprocessing

Each sample consists of a lookback window of length L with feature vector $\mathbf { x } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { F }$ that includes modulation/coding indicators and radio-quality metrics (e.g., modulation and coding scheme (MCS), channel quality indicator (CQI), rank indicator (RI), precoder matrix indicator (PMI), reference signal received quality (RSRQ), RSRP, received signal strength indicator (RSSI), and SINR), traffic/load indicators (e.g., buffer occupancy, SE, BLER, and delay), and the control variable used for planning (PRBs). Targets $\mathbf { y } _ { t + 1 }$ are one-stepahead KPIs; we report the univariate RSRP head and also train a full multivariate reconstruction head for representation learning. Standardization is per feature using mean and standard deviation computed on the training split only; the same affine maps are then frozen and applied to validation and test periods. Windows and labels are formed by sliding in time without overlap between the train/validation/test ranges. To remain indistribution during planning, admissible PRB values are clipped to the empirical [5, 95]th percentiles measured on the training set and mapped to the model’s standardized space.

## C. Model Hyperparameters

The world model couples a causal multi-scale SSM front end with a compact stochastic latent. We use the following configuration: embedding width d=192, SSM mixture layers $L _ { \ell } { = } 4$ , per-channel HiPPO–LegS state size N=64, mixture components M=4 with learned positive time steps (bilinear discretization), latent dimension $d _ { z } = 4 8$ , dropout 0.1, and lookback length L matching the recorded window. The target head is heteroscedastic (mean+log-variance clamped to [−8, 8]); a small autoregressive skip from the last input is combined through a bounded gain tanh(κ). Training uses AdamW with learning rate $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ , weight decay $1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ , batch size 256, cosine warm restarts stepped per mini-batch, gradient clipping at norm 1.0, and patience-based early stopping. We employ KL annealing from 0.01 to 1.0 over the first 20 epochs and scheduled mixing between posterior and prior encoders from 1.0 to 0.5 on the same schedule. Light input Gaussian noise (std 0.01) and feature-channel dropout (0.1) improve robustness. At test time, we average S=8 prior samples for the target prediction. For MPC/CEM planning, we set horizon $H { = } 8 ,$ , population 256, elite fraction 0.1, four CEM iterations, an action-smoothness penalty 0.05, and reward weights in standardized space that positively score SINR/SE/RSRP and penalize BLER/Delay/PRB usage.

To ensure fair comparisons, all neural baselines are matched in adequate capacity and trained with the same optimizer, early stopping, and chronological data splits. Inputs at time t contain only measurements available up to (and including) t, targets are at $t { + } 1$ , and standardization is fit on the training split only and frozen thereafter. Errors are reported in original physical units after inverse transforms; we additionally report latency per forward pass to reflect Near-RT feasibility. This protocol isolates architectural differences from data leakage and tuning effects, making the comparison both reproducible and operationally meaningful.

## D. Baselines

We evaluate the proposed world-modeling approach against baselines spanning state-space, attention, and recurrence inductive biases under leakage-safe, strictly causal evaluation. Concretely, we include: (a) a strictly multi-scale structured state-space mixture (MS<sup>3</sup>M [17]; SSM/HiPPO–LegS depthwise kernels with light channel mixing) that shares our causal front end but omits the stochastic latent and agentic rollouts—serving as a strong “pure SSM” reference isolating the value of compact latents for calibration and counterfactuals; (b) the recurrent–attention hybrid RWKV [23], which fuses token-bytoken recurrence with attention-style mixing and thus stresses exposure-bias behavior under one-step-ahead protocols; (c) a linear-attention Transformer (Performers [24]) that approximates softmax attention with kernel features to retain global content-based interactions at near-linear cost, suitable for near– real-time regimes (e.g., O-RAN telemetry); (d) a retentive state-space network (RetNet [25]) offering long-memory state updates and linear-time inference as a scalable SSM-style comparator; and (e) tokenized sequence models in the style of Chronos (Chronos-T5 / Chronos-GPT [26]), used here as decoder/seq2seq baselines trained from scratch on our dataset to probe the value of strong sequence priors even without unit-aware calibration.

To ensure fair timing/memory budgets, we use unified data processing and training for all models and implement compact baseline variants that preserve each method’s core inductive bias. These are not exact reproductions of the original codebases; the goal is comparability under a common setup rather than paper-by-paper replication.

## E. Why $W M { - } M S ^ { 3 } M$ Outperforms

In our results (Table II, Figure 3), each improvement maps to a concrete mechanism. The heteroscedastic target head plus a compact latent (with KL annealing and posterior–prior mixing) reduces typical errors without oversmoothing rare spikes, thus MAE decreases by 1.69% while RMSE remains essentially unchanged versus $\mathbf { M S } ^ { 3 } \mathbf { M } .$ The dual-decoder split (full-frame decoder + KPI-focused head with a bounded AR skip) together with a depthwise, strictly causal SSM backbone targets capacity where it matters, yielding ∼32% fewer parameters at the same latency. The telemetry-matched SSM inductive bias and a leakage-safe pipeline (chronological splits, train-only scalers) account for the 35–80% RMSE gains over attention/hybrid baselines while being 2.3–4.1× faster. In what-if planning, the model’s learned diminishing SINR/RSRP returns for higher PRBs, combined with the reward’s PRB/BLER/Delay penalties (Equation (34)) and in-distribution action bounds, make Step −20% PRB optimal on the evaluated window—explaining why WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M yields the specific outcomes we report.

## F. Operational Implications

Although the absolute error improvements over MS<sup>3</sup>M are modest, they are achieved with approximately 31.6 % fewer parameters and near-identical latency, which is valuable for edge-side deployment in Near-RT control loops. The large margins over attention, hybrid, and pretrained sequence models underscore that a causal, SSM-first inductive bias, combined with a calibrated latent, better matches O-RAN telemetry than token-centric or global-attention architectures. Beyond accuracy, the same substrate enables uncertainty-aware counterfactual analysis and short-horizon MPC/CEM planning without retraining, providing auditable decision support that extends past next-step prediction and aligns with safety and transparency requirements in 6G O-RAN.

## VI. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have proposed an agentic world modeling paradigm for 6G O-RAN that treats actions as first-class causes and plans over consequences under calibrated uncertainty. We have applied this approach with a PRB-conditioned WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M architecture that couples a strictly causal, multi-scale, and structured state-space front end with compact stochastic latent and dual decoders. The design enforces leakage-safe training, supports counterfactual (what-if) forecasting, and enables shorthorizon MPC/CEM planning inside data-driven operating range. On realistic O-RAN traces, WM–MS<sup>3</sup>M matched or surpassed strong sequence baselines while using fewer parameters and maintaining Near-RT latency. Beyond point accuracy, the model provides uncertainty summaries and auditable rollouts that better align with the safety and transparency requirements of radio control than LLM-centric approaches. Overall, the results indicate that world models with calibrated uncertainty can deliver control-grade prediction and decision support in O-RAN, while reserving LLMs for complementary roles (e.g., orchestration, explanation, policy templating) under strict grounding. We outline several directions to extend this study:

• Closed-loop field trials. Integrate the predictive and planning APIs with Near-RT RIC xApps/rApps and evaluate endto-end on live O-RAN segments, including safety guards, rollback, and human-in-the-loop adjudication.

• Richer controls and multi-cell coordination: Expand the action channel beyond PRBs (power, MCS, beams, timers) and model coordinated policies across cells/sectors with multi-agent rollouts and interference-aware rewards.

• Long-horizon and hierarchical planning: Combine shorthorizon MPC with value-function critics or options for hierarchical control; study horizon sensitivity and stability under receding-horizon execution.

• Adaptation and robustness: Add online/federated updates, change-point detection, and domain shift defenses (e.g., priors, adapters, ensembling) to maintain calibration under non-stationarity and vendor diversity.

• Causal structure and interpretability: Incorporate causal regularizers/latent interventions; expose sensitivities and counterfactual explanations at KPI and action levels for auditability and SLA diagnostics.

Pursuing these directions will move world-model-based control from accurate prediction and what-if analysis to safe, auditable, and scalable automation in open 6G networks.
