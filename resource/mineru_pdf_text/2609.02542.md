Abstract: Foothold-constrained terrain is characterized by sparse, discontinuous, or geometrically restricted feasible foot contacts, as encountered on stepping stones, across gaps, and on narrow stair treads. On such terrain, a single misstep often leaves little room to recover, so policies that base foot-placement decisions primarily on the immediately visible terrain are prone to failure. We ask whether a learned predictive summary of near-future observations and rewards can provide the anticipatory information required in such settings. We present World-Model-Augmented Visual Locomotion (WM-LOCO), which jointly trains a recurrent world model and a PPO policy. Conditioned on proprioception and a single onboard depth image, the world model produces a predictive recurrent feature that guides the policy, without explicit foothold labels. In simulation, WM-LOCO succeeds on gaps and stepping stones where a matched baseline fails completely, and matches the baseline’s success rate on stairs while improving stride efficiency and reducing pelvis acceleration. We deploy the same policy onboard a physical Unitree G1 humanoid using onboard proprioception and a single depth stream; it traverses all three terrain classes with an average success rate of 93.3%.

Keywords: Humanoid Locomotion, World Models, Foothold-Constrained Terrain, Reinforcement Learning

## 1 Introduction

Humans and other bipeds routinely look several steps ahead when crossing gaps, stairs, or stepping stones. They anticipate feasible contacts and select footholds before foot placement. By contrast, many reinforcement-learning-based locomotion policies are trained on continuous terrain, where feasible foot contacts are dense, and condition their actions on current or short-window observations [1, 2]. Recent humanoid systems that explicitly target sparse or challenging terrain therefore add dedicated foothold objectives, staged training, or privileged-to-visual distillation [3, 4]. Without such mechanisms, learned policies can perform well on continuous terrain, but struggle on terrain where only sparse, discontinuous, or geometrically restricted footholds are feasible, such as gaps, stepping stones, and narrow stair treads. We refer to such environments collectively as foothold-constrained terrain [5]. On such terrain, a single misstep is often enough to cause a fall [5], and the problem is especially hard for bipeds: compared with quadrupeds, bipeds have fewer supporting contacts available after a missed foothold.

This difficulty has motivated work on complex humanoid controllers for sparse footholds. Humanoid systems that traverse stepping stones or beams address the sparse foothold signal through complex mechanisms. BeamDojo splits dense and sparse objectives across critics and progressively increases terrain difficulty [3]. RPL first trains privileged experts and then distills them into a depth-based student [4]. PLANC wraps reduced-order references in a multi-phase teacher–distillation loop [6]. Model-based methods instead employ online mixed-integer quadratic programming for footstep planning [7]. These approaches demonstrate successful locomotion over sparse footholds, but typically rely on staged training, distillation, or model-based scaffolding. World models have been explored for predictive representation learning in legged locomotion. World-model-based perception learns a predictive latent for visual quadruped locomotion [8]. World models have also been used to reconstruct world states for humanoid control [9]. The Perceptive Internal Model approach has been applied to stair traversal [10]. DreamPolicy synthesizes future motion with diffusion [11]. These methods incorporate predictive or reconstructive objectives into locomotion control and report gains on stairs or rough ground. However, they do not explicitly focus on foothold-constrained terrain, nor measure whether the predictive signal helps when feasible footholds become sparse—a setting in which looking ahead is critical for bipeds.

To address this setting, we introduce World-Model-Augmented Visual Locomotion (WM-LOCO). Instead of staged critics, distillation, or planners, we co-train a recurrent world model with PPO end-to-end. The recurrent model summarizes predictive context from proprioception and a single onboard depth image, yielding a predictive recurrent feature without explicit foothold labels. Across three classes with qualitatively different foothold constraints—stairs, gaps, and stepping stones— WM-LOCO achieves consistently high success rates across the Easy, Medium, and Hard tiers, while the baseline reaches 0% on gaps and stepping stones at every tier. We further deploy the proposed method on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Running fully onboard, the policy traverses stepping stones, stairs, and a gap using proprioception and a single depth stream, with an average success rate of 93.3%.

## 2 Method

Our aim is to train a humanoid locomotion policy that can traverse terrain where the set of feasible foot contacts is sparse, discontinuous, or geometrically restricted—terrain on which a single infeasible foothold can cause termination. The proposed framework couples a recurrent world model with PPO to provide a predictive recurrent feature. The world model consumes proprioception and an egocentric depth image, and maintains a recurrent state that summarizes near-future observations and rewards. A feature $f _ { t } ^ { \operatorname { \breve { W } M } }$ derived from this recurrent state conditions the policy. The PPO policy then takes a proprioceptive history, a velocity command, and an egocentric depth image, together with $f _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { W M } }$ . A mixture-of-experts backbone fuses these inputs and feeds the actor and critic heads. The overall framework is illustrated in Figure 2. We detail the world-model formulation in Section 2.1. Section 2.2 describes the training rewards shared by all methods.

## 2.1 Forward-Aware World-Model Policy

Problem setup. We model locomotion as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). At each control step t the policy observes $o _ { t } = ( p _ { t } , d _ { t } , c _ { t } )$ , where $p _ { t }$ is a 5-frame proprioception history (joint positions, joint velocities, inertial measurements, and the previous action), $d _ { t }$ is a depth image from a single head-mounted onboard camera, and $c _ { t }$ is the commanded base-frame velocity. The task is to track $c _ { t }$ while traversing the terrain while avoiding non-foot contacts. We call terrain foothold-constrained when feasible foot-contact regions are sparse, discontinuous, or geometrically restricted relative to the robot’s nominal step length, so a single infeasible step can leave little room for recovery before the robot falls. Policies that condition foot-placement decisions primarily on the currently observed terrain are more susceptible to unrecoverable missteps—precisely the setting in which a predictive recurrent feature is expected to help.

World model. The WM-LOCO method processes $p _ { t }$ and $d _ { t }$ using a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) [12, 13] with a deterministic memory $h _ { t }$ and a stochastic latent $z _ { t }$ (dimension 128). Intuitively, $h _ { t }$ is updated recurrently from the preceding state, latent, and action, while $z _ { t }$ captures observation-dependent information beyond that represented by the deterministic state. Concretely, the model learns (i) a transition $h _ { t } = f _ { \phi } ( h _ { t - 1 } , z _ { t - 1 } , a _ { t - 1 } )$ that updates the memory with the last action, (ii) a posterior $z _ { t } \sim q _ { \phi } ( z _ { t } \mid h _ { t } , p _ { t } , d _ { t } )$ that infers $z _ { t }$ from the memory and the current observation, (iii) a prior $\hat { z _ { t } } \sim p _ { \phi } ( z _ { t } \mid h _ { t } )$ that predicts $z _ { t }$ from the memory without the current observation, and (iv) decoders that reconstruct proprioception, depth, and reward. The training objective is

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{WM}} = \underbrace {\operatorname{MSE} (\hat {p} _ {t} , p _ {t})} _ {\text {proprio recon}} + \underbrace {\operatorname{MSE} (\hat {d} _ {t} , d _ {t})} _ {\text {depth recon}} + \underbrace {\operatorname{MSE} (\hat {r} _ {t} , r _ {t})} _ {\text {reward pred}} + \beta \underbrace {\operatorname{KL} (q _ {\phi} \| p _ {\phi})} _ {\text {forward prediction}}.\tag{1}
$$

The first three terms encourage the latent to retain information about the observations and rewards. The KL term regularizes the prior toward the posterior, thereby encouraging $h _ { t }$ to retain information useful for predicting the latent before the current observation arrives.

The world model is trained jointly with the policy rather than in a separate pretraining stage. We optimize the standard PPO clipped surrogate objective together with ${ \mathcal { L } } _ { \mathrm { W M } }$ in a single joint update, and propagate world-model gradients into the shared depth and proprioceptive encoders. Only the memory is passed to the policy as $f _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { W M } } = g _ { \psi } ( h _ { t } )$ . Inference does not require imagined rollouts, and no foothold labels are used.

## 2.2 Training Reward

A standard locomotion objective—velocity tracking plus standard gait shaping—is too sparse for foothold-constrained terrain: most rollouts terminate before the agent has discovered any signal that distinguishes a feasible step from an infeasible one, and the irreversibility of an early misstep makes temporal credit assignment from the eventual fall difficult. We therefore augment the standard reward $r _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { s t d } }$ with terrain-specific shaping terms, applied identically to WM-LOCO and PPO within each terrain-training setting. The terrain-specific contact terms described below are computed from the foot volume-point set $\mathcal { V } _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { f o o t } }$ —contact points sampled on each foot mesh and propagated to the world frame every control step, following the sensor design of Zhu et al. [14]. The full term-by-term schedule with weights is in Section B.

Stair-boundary penalty. Two-dimensional penalty zones flank each stair structure on the surrounding flat ground, discouraging the policy from detouring around the terrain to satisfy velocity tracking without engaging the foothold constraint. The corresponding nonnegative violation term counts foot sample points inside any such zone,

$$
r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{bdry}} = \big | \mathcal {V} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{foot}} \cap \mathcal {Z} ^ {\mathrm{bdry}} \big |,\tag{2}
$$

where ${ \mathcal { Z } } ^ { \mathrm { b d r y } }$ is a per-tile union of 2D rectangles beside the stair footprint (the tile’s full lateral range × an x-strip flanking the footprint). The edge-zone construction follows the Terrain Edge Detection primitive of Zhu et al. [14]; we apply it to the flat ground surrounding a stair tile rather than to terrain edges directly.

Riser-penetration penalty. The corresponding nonnegative per-step violation term counts foot sample points that fall inside any stair riser,

$$
r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{riser}} = \big | \mathcal {V} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{foot}} \cap \mathcal {Z} ^ {\mathrm{riser}} \big |,\tag{3}
$$

where ${ \mathcal { Z } } ^ { \mathrm { r i s e r } }$ is a per-tile union of axis-aligned 3D cuboids over each riser face, with the upper 4 cm of each riser excluded. This clearance prevents oblique foot contacts near the leading edge of the tread from being misclassified as riser penetrations. Without this penalty, we observe consistent riser strikes in simulation; riser strikes were also observed during hardware testing. With the penalty, this failure is not observed in our simulation rollouts, and the resulting policy transfers to hardware without further tuning.

Sequential stair-tread contact reward. The reward maintains an ordered sequence of horizontal stair-tread contact regions $R _ { 1 } , R _ { 2 } , . . .$ . in the robot’s current heading, and a per-environment index $k _ { t }$ marks the “current” region. When a foot first attains a sole-contact ratio of $c _ { \mathrm { m i n } } = 0 . 5$ on $R _ { k _ { t } }$ a velocity-adaptive activation window of $\tau _ { t } = \mathrm { c l i p } ( \ell _ { k _ { t } } / ( 2 \bar { v } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } \Delta t ) , 5 , 5 0 )$ control steps opens, where $\ell _ { k _ { t } }$ is the region depth and $\bar { v } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } }$ the commanded forward speed. During the open window,

$$
r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{tread}} = g _ {t} \rho_ {t} ^ {\mathrm{seq}} - 0. 7 \max \bigl (0, \rho_ {t} ^ {\mathrm{other}} - 0. 0 5 \bigr),\tag{4}
$$

where $g _ { t } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ is 1 while the window of $R _ { k _ { t } }$ is open, $\rho _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { s e q } }$ is the sole-contact ratio of the foot required by left–right alternation on $R _ { k _ { t } }$ (a wrong-foot landing receives credit discounted by a factor of 0.3 rather than zero, thereby preserving a nonzero learning signal), and $\rho _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { o t h e r } }$ is the other foot’s overlap with the current region. The index advances to $R _ { k _ { t } + 1 }$ only after contact is sustained for at least $0 . 3 \tau _ { t }$ frames; contacts with subsequent regions receive no reward until the current region is completed.

Stepping-stone top-contact reward. Stepping stones use a separate parallel formulation: all valid stone-top regions for the current tile are active at reset, and each region is consumed independently after qualifying contact. Thus, there is no sequential ordering or alternating-foot logic. Center-plateau shaping encourages centered footholds, while per-foot lane gating penalizes wrong-foot overlap on lane-tagged stones.

Total reward. For the gap+stairs training setting, the three terms combine with the standard reward as

$$
r _ {t} = r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{std}} + w _ {b} r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{bdry}} + w _ {r} r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{riser}} + w _ {t} r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{tread}},\tag{5}
$$

with weights $w _ { b } , w _ { r } , w _ { t }$ listed in Section B. The three terms were tuned jointly with the world-model loss.

## 3 Experiments

We compare our framework against a PPO baseline in simulation, examine how performance varies with the degree of foothold constraint, and validate the trained policy on hardware.

Setup. We simulate a Unitree G1 humanoid in IsaacLab with 8192 parallel environments on a single NVIDIA RTX 5880-Ada (48 GB). Depth observations are obtained from a simulated headmounted Intel RealSense D435. Images are rendered at 64 × 36 with a $8 9 . 5 ^ { \circ } \times 5 8 . 3 ^ { \circ }$ field of view, cropped to $3 2 \times 1 8$ , clipped to 0.1–2.5 m, and sampled at 50 Hz. Stairs, gaps, and stepping stones are generated from procedural parameter ranges listed in Section A. Both methods are trained for the same number of iterations with massively parallel PPO [1]. The world-model loss ${ \mathcal { L } } _ { \mathrm { W M } }$ is added to the PPO surrogate as a single joint objective without an offline replay buffer. The design of the foot volume-point sensor and flat-patch terrain sampler follows Hiking in the Wild [14]. We implement the world-model pathway, foothold-constrained terrain generators, and the evaluation protocol used in this paper.

Baseline. We compare WM-LOCO against a PPO baseline under matched training conditions. Both methods share the same reward, perception stream, proprioceptive encoder, AMP motion prior, MoE actor-critic, and iteration budget. The baseline omits only the world-model pathway and its auxiliary loss; therefore, the observed differences suggest a contribution from the predictive recurrent feature.

Evaluation protocol. Each terrain class is evaluated on held-out terrain instances. We test one class at a time rather than mixing classes in a single episode. For each terrain class and difficulty tier, we fix the terrain at the corresponding generator row, disable external push perturbations and domain randomization so the comparison focuses on traversal rather than disturbance recovery, and collect at least 50 episodes per (method, terrain, difficulty) setting. An episode is counted as a success if the robot’s center of mass crosses the goal line within 45 s; otherwise it is a failure.

Metrics. The primary metric is success rate: the fraction of episodes in which the robot’s center of mass crosses the goal line within the time limit. On stairs, we evaluate gait quality using stride length, steps per meter, normalized mechanical energy consumption, pelvis acceleration, and action rate (Section E).

## 4 Results

Success rates. Table 1 reports success rates at three difficulty levels: Easy, Medium, and Hard. Each reported number corresponds to one fixed difficulty level. On gaps and stepping stones the performance difference is substantial: the PPO baseline reaches 0.0% success at every level, while WM-LOCO reaches between 78.2% (stepping stones, Hard) and 100% (gap, Medium). On stairs, PPO achieves success rates between 87.0% and 91.4%, whereas WM-LOCO achieves rates between 92.0% and 95.7%; the observed differences are no more than 7.2 percentage points. Overall, the performance gap is modest on stairs but widens on gaps and stepping stones, where PPO reaches 0% at every difficulty tier while WM-LOCO remains above 78%.

Gait quality on stairs. On stairs both methods reach similar success rates, but they differ in gait quality. WM-LOCO exhibits longer strides (stride length +15% to +35%), fewer steps per meter (−9% to −21%), and lower pelvis acceleration (−24% to −33%). It also reduces normalized mechanical energy consumption by 6%–20%. Full results are in Section E.

Failure modes on stepping stones and gaps. On stepping stones, WM-LOCO succeeds in 78.2%– 88.3% of episodes, while the baseline reaches 0.0% at every difficulty level. When WM-LOCO fails, the dominant mode is an illegal foothold (23.0%, aggregated over levels) rather than a fall (1.9%). Baseline failures are mostly falls (61.0%) or lack of progress (36.0%), often within the first few contacts near the beginning of an episode. A similar pattern is observed on gaps: the baseline stays at 0% across levels, while WM-LOCO remains at or above 90%.

Posterior reconstruction quality. After one warm-up step, the per-step depth reconstruction MSE is generally below $1 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ , apart from a transient peak of approximately $1 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ near steps 45–55 on [0, 1]-normalized depth. With command resampling disabled, we report the per-tick MSE as the median across 7 parallel environments to suppress single-environment reset transients (Figure 3; per-panel breakdown in Section D). The world-model head therefore learns a faithful multimodal embedding of the observation stream associated with the success-rate gains reported above. This analysis characterizes encoder/decoder reconstruction fidelity rather than open-loop forward-prediction quality.

Sim-to-real evaluation. We export the WM-LOCO policy to ONNX and deploy it on a Unitree G1 humanoid. The policy runs fully onboard a Jetson Orin from proprioception and a single headmounted depth stream, using an Intel RealSense D435 corresponding to the camera model used in simulation. The system requires no offboard perception, precomputed terrain map, or additional state estimator (Figure 1). On stepping stones with 0.25 m-wide top surfaces and 0.45 m longitudinal gaps, the policy successfully traverses the discrete pads without observed foot-placement failures. On stairs with 0.15 m risers and 0.25 m treads, the robot negotiates each riser and places each foot flat on the tread without additional tuning. The robot traverses the 0.8 m gap in a single step, exhibiting a swing-and-landing pattern similar to that observed in simulation. We do not test larger gaps for safety reasons. ## 5 Limitations

Scope. The framework targets foothold-constrained terrain where feasible contacts are dominated by discrete pads or narrow geometric constraints. Continuous narrow supports (balance beams) and stepping stones with varying elevations combine lateral and vertical constraints and may require separate reward shaping; outdoor unstructured terrain is beyond the scope of this study. Platform and architecture. Results are reported on a single Unitree G1 humanoid robot and a single RSSM world-model backbone [13, 8]. The policy consumes a generic predictive recurrent feature, allowing the formulation to accommodate alternative world-model architectures. Evaluating other humanoid morphologies and additional foothold-constrained tasks remains an important direction for future work.

## 6 Conclusion

We present WM-LOCO, a visual humanoid locomotion framework for foothold-constrained terrain. A recurrent world model, jointly trained with PPO, supplies a predictive recurrent feature to the actor–critic using onboard proprioception and depth. On the evaluated stepping-stone and gap classes, WM-LOCO achieves high success rates, whereas the PPO baseline records no successful trials; on stairs both methods succeed, and WM-LOCO improves stride efficiency and reduces pelvis acceleration. The same policy runs onboard a Jetson Orin. In real-time hardware experiments, the robot exhibits contact patterns similar to those observed in simulation across all three terrain classes.

## C Training and Architecture

Both methods are trained with PPO using the same hyperparameters and training budget. WM-LOCO additionally includes the RSSM pathway, its auxiliary objective, and the recurrent feature supplied to the policy. The world-model component adopts the RSSM formulation [13] and is optimized jointly with the policy following WMP [8]. The terrain curriculum advances independently for each environment according to exponential velocity-tracking scores: an environment moves up one difficulty tile when both $r _ { \mathrm { e x p } } ^ { \mathrm { x y } } > 0 . 7 5$ and $r _ { \mathrm { e x p } } ^ { \mathrm { y a w } } > 0 . 5$ , and moves down when $r _ { \mathrm { e x p } } ^ { \mathrm { x y } } < 0 . 5$
