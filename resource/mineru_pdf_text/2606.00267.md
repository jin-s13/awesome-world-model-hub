Abstract: Video world models (WMs) have shown promise for policy evaluation and improvement by imagining realistic future observations conditioned on ego-robot actions. While WMs can model distributions over futures, policy evaluation and improvement typically rely on nominal imaginations, which can miss high-impact outcomes of robot actions unless prohibitively many samples are drawn. To enable robust policy evaluation and improvement over WM imaginations, we propose STRESSDREAM, which steers imaginations toward highimpact yet plausible outcomes specified at inference time by optimizing the initial noise of diffusion-based WMs. However, optimizing high-dimensional noise is challenging: the optimization must reason about nuanced, scene-dependent target events in generated videos while avoiding out-of-distribution (OOD) noise that yields implausible imaginations. We address this with two complementary objectives: a semantic objective with a Vision-Language Model that provides informative gradients by reasoning about the generated video, and a plausibility objective that prevents the optimized noise from drifting OOD. With state-of-the-art video world models for autonomous driving and robotic manipulation, we show that STRESSDREAM effectively steers imaginations toward high-impact yet plausible outcomes specified by text at inference time, such as task failures, enabling robust policy evaluation and improvement by identifying actions whose plausible futures include undesirable outcomes.

Keywords: World Models, Inference-time Steering, Policy Learning and Evaluation

## 1 Introduction

Video world models (WMs), or learned simulators of physical environments, have attracted significant interest in robotics, promising policy evaluation and improvement with substantially less costly real-world interaction [1, 2, 3]. At their core, video WMs are generative models, such as diffusion and flow matching [4, 5, 6], learning distributions over future observations conditioned on ego robot actions, and thereby capturing the inherent uncertainty of physical interactions or behaviors of other agents [7, 8, 9, 10] (top, Fig. 1). For example, in robotic manipulation, dropping an open bag from high above the table may or may not cause its contents to spill, leading to either task success or failure; in autonomous driving, the same ego-vehicle decision can elicit qualitatively different outcomes depending on the responses of surrounding agents, such as braking or maintaining speed.

Despite learning rich distributions over future outcomes, policy evaluation and improvement with WMs typically rely on nominal imaginations [8, 11, 12, 13], which can under-explore the diverse outcomes they are trained to model. Naively sampling imaginations from WMs can easily miss critical outcomes without drawing prohibitively many samples, making it ill-suited to the role of WMs: exploring plausible, high-impact outcomes of robot actions for policy evaluation and improvement [14, 15, 16, 17], where plausibility refers to outcomes supported by the learned WM distribution. For example, when a manipulator drops an open bag from high above the table, the WM’s distribution may include outcomes both with and without spilling; in contrast, when the bag is placed close to the table, spilling may be unlikely or absent from the outcome distribution (bottom, Fig. 1). The ability to imagine plausible, high-impact outcomes can therefore allow the latter action to be preferred during policy evaluation, while the former is downweighted or suppressed during policy improvement. This motivates the central question of our work:

## How can we efficiently steer video world models at inference time to imagine plausible, high-impact outcomes for policy evaluation and improvement?

Our key idea is to optimize the initial noise of a diffusion-based WM, steering generation toward high-impact events specified at inference time (e.g., spills or collisions), within the distribution of plausible outcomes. The challenge, however, is to guide this optimization in extremely highdimensional noise spaces (e.g., 1M dims [11]): the optimization must reason about nuanced, scenedependent target events from generated videos, while naive optimization can easily push the initial noise into out-of-distribution (OOD) regions [18, 19], producing implausible videos. To address this, we propose STRESSDREAM, which combines a semantic objective, using a Vision-Language Model (VLM) that reasons about what happens in the generated video to guide generation toward target events, with a plausibility objective that keeps the optimized noise within the in-distribution region. Together, STRESSDREAM provides dense gradient signals for searching the high-dimensional noise space, while preserving plausibility of generations, enabling video WMs to explore plausible, highimpact outcome distributions for robust policy evaluation and improvement.

Statement of Contributions. We propose STRESSDREAM, a novel inference-time steering method for diffusion-based video world models. Our algorithm optimizes the high-dimensional Gaussian initial noise to steer generations toward high-impact outcomes while preserving plausibility. Through experiments, we show: (i) in controlled settings with access to the true system dynamics, STRESSDREAM steers WM imaginations toward target task-failure events only when such outcomes are plausible; (ii) on state-of-the-art video world models for autonomous driving [11] and robotic manipulation [12], STRESSDREAM enables robust policy evaluation by detecting high-impact outcomes, such as task failures, with substantially higher recall $( 5 4 \%  9 4 \% )$ ; and (iii) this robust evaluation improves policy by promoting robust actions that avoid potential failures, increasing the success rate of a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy [20] (39% → 71%).

## 2 Background: Action-conditioned Video World Models

Video World Models. An action-conditioned video world model $f _ { \theta } ,$ parameterized by $\theta ,$ models distributions of future observations $\mathbf { o _ { \lambda } } : = o _ { t : t + H } ,$ , where t denotes the time and H is the prediction horizon [12, 15, 16, 21, 22, 23]. Each observation $o = [ I ^ { 1 } , \cdot \cdot \cdot , I ^ { n } , q ] \in \mathcal { O }$ consists of n camera images $I ^ { i }$ and proprioceptive state $q ,$ such as the robot joint configuration. The prediction is conditioned on an observation history $\mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } : = o _ { t - h : t }$ and a future ego-action sequence $\mathbf { a } : = a _ { t : t + H } \colon$

$$
\mathbf {o} \sim f _ {\theta} (\cdot \mid \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }}, \mathbf {a}), \qquad \text { where } \quad \mathbf {o} := o _ {t: t + H}, \quad \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }} := o _ {t - h: t}, \quad \mathbf {a} := a _ {t: t + H}.\tag{1}
$$

Diffusion Models as Video World Models. Recent video world models [11, 12, 23] often instantiate $f _ { \theta }$ as a diffusion model [4, 24], learning a transformation between the data distribution, $\mathbf { x } ^ { 0 } \ : = \ \mathbf { o } \ \sim \ p ^ { \mathrm { d a t a } } ( \mathbf { o } )$ , and a tractable noise distribution, typically a standard Gaussian $\mathbf { x } ^ { T } : = \boldsymbol { \epsilon } \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ , where $D$ is a noise dimension, indexed by diffusion time $\tau \in \{ 0 , 1 , \ldots , T \}$

$$
\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau} = \alpha^ {\tau} \mathbf {x} + \sigma^ {\tau} \boldsymbol {\epsilon}, \quad \text { where } \quad \mathbf {x} ^ {0} := \mathbf {o}, \quad \mathbf {x} ^ {T} := \boldsymbol {\epsilon} \sim \mathcal {N} (\mathbf {0}, \mathbf {I} _ {D}),\tag{2}
$$

where $\alpha ^ { \tau }$ is a decreasing function of $\mathit { \Delta } _ { \tau }$ and $\sigma ^ { \tau }$ is an increasing function. Generation proceeds in the reverse direction by iteratively mapping a noisier sample to one at a less noisy diffusion time :

$$
d ^ {\tau} \left(\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}, \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }}, \mathbf {a}\right) := \mathbf {x} ^ {\tau - 1} = \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \left(\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau} - \sigma^ {\tau} g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} \left(\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}, \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }}, \mathbf {a}\right)\right) + \sigma^ {\tau - 1} g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} \left(\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}, \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }}, \mathbf {a}\right),\tag{3}
$$

where $g _ { \theta } ^ { \tau }$ is the noise predictor (see Appendix $\mathrm { A } )$ . Starting from an initial Gaussian sample ${ \mathbf x } ^ { T } = \epsilon$ repeated application of this reverse update produces the predicted future observations $\mathbf { x } ^ { 0 } = \mathbf { o }$

Initial Noise as a Control Variable. Following (3), which corresponds to the probability-flow ODE [25], for fixed conditioning inputs $( \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } , \mathbf { a } )$ , the imaginations are a deterministic function of the initial noise: $\mathbf { o } = f _ { \theta } ( \epsilon , \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } , \mathbf { a } )$ . Thus, all stochasticity in generation is governed by the initial noise, implying that selecting the initial noise controls which video is generated from the WMs.

## 3 Setup: Policy Evaluation and Improvement with Video World Models

Policy Evaluation and Improvement with Video World Models. Given an action-conditioned video world model $f _ { \theta } ,$ we aim to evaluate candidate action sequences by imagining their future outcomes, which can then be used for policy evaluation [15, 16, 21] and improvement [12, 13, 17]. The predicted futures o are evaluated by a test-time criterion function $\mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { t e s t } } ( \mathbf { o } ) \in \mathbb { R }$ , which measures whether plausible outcomes with high impact on policy evaluation and improvement occur in the generated videos. These evaluations can be used to improve policies by selecting actions that avoid such outcomes [26, 27], or by training a policy $\pi ( \mathbf { a } \mid \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } )$ using world-model imaginations [12, 13].

However, when multiple futures are possible under the same action sequence, robust policy [28, 29] should account for the distribution of outcomes that are plausible under the ego robot action:

Inner opt.: high-dim. (e.g., D ≈1M) Gaussian noise generating high-impact, yet plausible futures

$$
\mathbf {a} ^ {*} = \underset {\mathbf {a} \in \mathcal {A}} {\operatorname{argmin}} \max _ {\epsilon \in \mathbb {R} ^ {D}} \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { test }} (f _ {\theta} (\epsilon , \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }}, \mathbf {a}))\tag{4}
$$

where the inner optimization chooses a Gaussian noise to steer the WM generation toward a worstcase plausible future that maximizes the criterion, while the outer optimization selects a robust action sequence that keeps the criterion low across plausible futures, even under worst-case outcomes. Various methods can be used for the outer optimization: $\pmb { A }$ may be a discrete set in a sampling-based solver [26], or a continuous action space over which a policy is optimized [13]. However, solving the inner optimization is challenging, as it requires optimizing over a high-dimensional noise space.

Steering Imagination via Inference-time Noise Optimization. Since the initial noise acts as a control variable for the generation of WMs (Sec. 2), the goal of the inner optimization corresponds to steering the WM imagination toward a high-impact outcome that remains plausible under the learned model. However, because evaluating each noise requires an expensive denoising process, repeated random sampling is highly inefficient and likely to miss rare but plausible critical outcomes within a limited sampling budget. Instead, we leverage the gradient of a differentiable criterion function to steer generation more efficiently, optimizing the initial noise directly via gradient ascent:

$$
\boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {i + 1} = \boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {i} + \eta \nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {i}} \left[ \mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{test}} (\mathbf {o} _ {i}) \right], \quad \text { where } \quad \mathbf {o} _ {i} = f _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {i}, \mathbf {o} ^ {\mathrm{hist}}, \mathbf {a}),\tag{5}
$$

where $\eta$ denotes a step size. Although this first-order gradient ascent requires a small number of iterative updates, each involving forward and backward passes through both the WM and the criterion function, the dense gradient signal makes it substantially more efficient than zeroth-order random sampling for discovering noises that induce plausible, high-impact video generations [19, 30, 31].

Challenge: High-Dimensional Noise Space. While gradient-based optimization in (5) can steer diffusion model generation at inference time [19, 31], applying it to video world models is challenging because their noise spaces are extremely high-dimensional, $\mathrm { e . g . , \approx 1 M }$ in driving [11] and ≈ 50K in manipulation [12]. The optimization must guide generation toward meaningful, scenedependent target events while preserving plausibility under the learned WM distribution. However, naive optimization can easily push the noise into out-of-distribution (OOD) regions of Gaussian noises, producing implausible videos (Fig. 1), and the test-time criterion ${ \mathcal { C } } ^ { \mathrm { t e s t } }$ must be differentiable while reasoning about nuanced, task-relevant events that vary across scenes. Moreover, solving (5) is inefficient because exact gradients require backpropagating through the iterative denoising process.

## 4 STRESSDREAM: Steering High-Dimensional Video World Models

To address the challenges of steering video world models, we propose STRESSDREAM, which defines a differentiable optimization criterion by combining a semantic objective using Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with a plausibility objective that prevents OOD noise: $\mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { t e s t } } = \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { s e m } } + \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { p l a } }$ The semantic objective uses inference-time text specifications of high-impact outcomes to reason about whether the target events occur in the generated video (Sec. 4.1). The plausibility objective preserves the statistical properties of the standard Gaussian noise prior, thereby maintaining the plausibility of WM generations (Sec. 4.2). Finally, we approximate the noise gradient to avoid full backpropagation through the iterative denoising process, enabling efficient optimization (Sec. 4.3).

## 4.1 Semantic Objective: Steering towards Target Events with VLM Gradients

Since video WMs operate across diverse scenes and tasks, the high-impact target events for steering may change with the policy context, requiring a semantic objective that can differentiably score whether scene-dependent target events occur in generated videos. We therefore leverage the general video-understanding capability of a Vision-Language Model (VLM) as the semantic objective. Given an inference-time text prompt l describing the target high-impact outcome, the VLM measures how well the generated video o matches the prompt. For example, in robotic manipulation, one may specify “the coffee beans spill,” while in driving, one may specify “a collision occurs.”

To obtain a differentiable score, we use Qwen-VL [32] and prompt it to output a single token, yes or no, indicating whether o matches l. We then define $\mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { s e m } }$ as the difference in log token probabilities:

$$
\mathcal {C} ^ {\text { sem }} (\mathbf {o}; \boldsymbol {l}) = \log p ^ {\text { VLM }} (\text { yes } \mid \mathbf {o}, \boldsymbol {l}) - \log p ^ {\text { VLM }} (\text { no } \mid \mathbf {o}, \boldsymbol {l}).\tag{6}
$$

Using the VLM’s single-token probabilities makes the objective differentiable, providing rich gradient signals for optimizing high-dimensional noise that generates an inference-time target event.

## 4.2 Plausibility Objective: Keeping High-dimensional Noise in the Typical Set

Diffusion models are trained to generate samples from noise drawn from a Gaussian prior. When the noise leaves the typical set [33, 34, 35], where most training-time noise samples lie, the resulting outof-distribution (OOD) noise can produce implausible videos that are unlikely under the WM distribution or have degraded visual quality. Importantly, in high dimensions, the typical set is not the same as the region of highest probability density: some noise vectors, such as the zero vector, may have high density but are extremely unlikely to be sampled from the Gaussian prior (See Appendix B.1 for more details). Since gradient-based noise optimization can perturb the noise away from this typical set [18, 19, 31, 36, 37], we employ a plausibility objective that encourages the noise to remain within the typical set of the Gaussian prior, with a combination of functions of the noise that capture key statistics of high-dimensional Gaussian noise: $\mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { p l a } } ( \epsilon ) = \lambda _ { 1 } \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } } ( \epsilon ) + \lambda _ { 2 } \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { i s o } } ( \epsilon ) + \lambda _ { 3 } \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { s p e c } } ( \epsilon )$

Norm Concentration. First, since the squared norm of Gaussian noise follows a chi-square distribution, $\| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } \sim \chi _ { D } ^ { 2 }$ , it concentrates sharply around the noise dimension D [18]. We therefore regularize deviations of the noise radius from the typical Gaussian shell: $\mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } } ( \epsilon ) : = - \left( \| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } - \sqrt { D } \right) ^ { 2 }$

Isotropy. While norm concentration enforces typicality at a global scale, the optimized noise may still contain local correlations or structured coordinate patterns that are unlikely under an i.i.d. Gaussian prior [19]. To encourage coordinate-wise independence, we randomly permute the entries of ϵ and partition them into m subvectors $\{ \epsilon _ { i } \} _ { i = 1 } ^ { m } .$ , where $\epsilon _ { i } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { k }$ and $D = m k$ . Under the Gaussian prior, these subvectors should behave as i.i.d. samples from $\mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { k } )$ , and therefore their empirical second moment $\begin{array} { r } { \widehat { \pmb { \Sigma } } = \frac { 1 } { m } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { m } \epsilon _ { i } \pmb { \epsilon } _ { i } ^ { \top } } \end{array}$ should be close to $\mathbf { I } _ { k }$ . We therefore penalize deviations from blockwise isotropy as $\begin{array} { r } { \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { i s o } } ( \epsilon ) : = - \frac { 1 } { k } \| \widehat { \boldsymbol { \Sigma } } - \mathbf { I } _ { k } \| _ { F } ^ { 2 } } \end{array}$ , averaged over multiple random permutations.

Spectral Whiteness. Even when the noise remains typical in coordinate space, optimization can introduce frequency-domain artifacts [38]. Since Gaussian noise has a flat expected power spectrum [39, 40], we encourage the noise to remain spectrally white. Let $\mathcal { F }$ denote a unitary 2D discrete Fourier transform applied over the spatial dimensions of each noise slice, and compute the power spectrum $\mathbf { P } = | \mathcal { F } ( \bar { \epsilon } ) | ^ { 2 }$ . We aggregate P into B spatial-frequency bins to obtain bin-averaged powers $\{ \widehat { p } _ { b } \} _ { b = 1 } ^ { B }$ . To preserve spectral flatness, we minimize the variance of these bin-averaged powers, $\begin{array} { r } { \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { s p e c } } ( \epsilon ) : = - \frac { 1 } { B } \sum _ { b = 1 } ^ { B } ( \widehat { p } _ { b } - \bar { p } ) ^ { 2 } } \end{array}$ , where $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { p } = \frac { 1 } { B } \sum _ { b ^ { \prime } = 1 } ^ { B } \widehat { p } _ { b ^ { \prime } } } \end{array}$ is the mean power across bins.

## 4.3 Approximating Noise Gradients for Efficient Steering

Noise optimization in (5) requires computing the gradient of the criterion with respect to the initial noise, $\bar { \nabla } _ { \epsilon } \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { t e s t } } \big ( f _ { \theta } ( \epsilon , \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } , \mathbf { a } ) \bar { \big ) } = \nabla _ { \epsilon } \mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { t e s t } } \big ( d ^ { 1 } \bar { \big ( } \cdot \cdot \cdot d ^ { T } ( \epsilon , \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } , \mathbf { a } ) \big ) \big )$ , where $d ^ { \tau } ( \cdot )$ denotes one denoising step in (3). Although diffusion models are differentiable in principle, backpropagating through the full iterative denoising process (e.g., 50 steps [41]) can incur high memory cost and vanishing gradient [42, 43]. To make optimization efficient, we adopt a score-distillation [43] (Appendix B.4), which approximates the gradient with respect to the noise using the gradient at the generated sample:

$$
\nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\epsilon}} \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { test }} (\mathbf {o}) \approx \beta   \nabla_ {\mathbf {o}} \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { test }} (\mathbf {o}), \quad \text { where } \quad \mathbf {o} = f _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}, \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }}, \mathbf {a}) = d ^ {1} \big (\dots d ^ {T} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}, \mathbf {o} ^ {\text { hist }}, \mathbf {a}) \big),\tag{7}
$$

where $\beta$ is a scalar coefficient. This approximation only requires backpropagation through the differentiable criterion function, avoiding full backpropagation through the iterative denoising process.

In sum, STRESSDREAM optimizes the initial noise as in (5), using a gradient that combines the gradient approximation, the semantic objective (VLM), and the plausibility objective (typical set):

$$
\nabla_ {\epsilon} \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { test }} (\mathbf {o}) = \beta \nabla_ {\mathbf {o}} \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { sem }} (\mathbf {o}; l) + \nabla_ {\epsilon} \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { pla }} (\epsilon),\tag{8}
$$

where the coefficients $\beta , \lambda _ { 1 } , \lambda _ { 2 } , \lambda _ { 3 }$ are tuned depending on the WM, noise dimension, and VLMs.

## 5 Case Study: Steering Dubins Car Video World Model

We first study STRESSDREAM in controlled settings where the system dynamics and uncertainty are known. Since we know the true dynamics, this setup allows us to test whether WM steering can effectively identify high-impact futures (i.e., failures) only when they are possible under the system.

Naughty 3D Dubins Car. We consider an image-based setup of a discrete-time 3D Dubins car with state $s = [ p _ { x } , p _ { y } , \theta ]$ , continuous angular-velocity action $a _ { t } \in [ - 1 . 2 5 , 1 . 2 5 ] \mathrm { r a d / s } .$ fixed speed $v = 1 \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { s }$ , and timestep $\Delta t = 0 . 0 5 \mathrm { s }$ . The naughty Dubins car introduces uncertainty by randomly flipping the sign of the control input: $\delta _ { t } = - 1$ with probability $p = 0 . 2$ , and $\delta _ { t } = 1$ otherwise:

$$
s _ {t + 1} = s _ {t} + \Delta t [ v \cos (\theta_ {t}), v \sin (\theta_ {t}), \delta_ {t} a _ {t} ], \quad \delta_ {t} \in \{- 1, 1 \}.\tag{9}
$$

Each state is rendered as a $3 \times 1 2 8 \times \cdot$ 128 RGB image showing the environment and vehicle. In this setup, instead of the VLM-based $\mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { s e m } }$ , we use the safety score defined as $\mathcal { C } ( s ) = p _ { x } ^ { 2 } + p _ { y } ^ { 2 } - 0 . 2 5 ^ { 2 }$ which induces a circular failure set of radius 0.25 m centered at the origin: ${ \mathcal { F } } : = \{ s \ | \ { \mathcal { C } } ( { \bar { s } } ) \leq 0 \}$

Video World Model. We train a WM on an offline dataset of 4,000 random observation–action trajectories. We train a safety-score predictor to regress the ground-truth score from an image, and train a diffusion world model, $f _ { \theta } ( o _ { t + 1 } \mid \epsilon , o _ { t } , a _ { t } )$ , to generate one-step future observation (H = 1) conditioned on the current observation and action. The resulting noise dimension is 1024.

Experiment Setup. We aim to detect action sequences that have a possibility of entering the failure set using WM imaginations. We construct an evaluation dataset of 5,000 random initial state and action sequences. For each trajectory, we compute the ground-truth minimum safety score achievable under the stochastic dynamics in (9), and label it as positive if the g.t. minimum score is below zero, indicating that the trajectory can enter the failure set. We then perform open-loop world-model rollouts using only the initial image $o _ { 0 }$ and the action sequence, and classify an imagined trajectory as a failure, i.e., positive, if its minimum predicted safety score along the rollout is below zero.

STRESSDREAM plausibly generates videos aligned with inference-time specifications of target safetycritical or failure events that nominal imaginations miss, enabling robust evaluation of action sequences through WM imaginations. Fig. 4 shows that, without the typical-set constraint, both target-alignment and video-quality scores decrease, indicating that the plausibility objective helps preserve plausibility. For manipulation, STRESSDREAM detects task-failure events in imagination with higher recall, whereas random generations are often overly optimistic and miss possible failures, as shown in Fig. 5. ## 6.2 Policy Improvement with Steered Video World Model Imaginations

Setup. We study whether STRESSDREAM can improve a robotic policy through steered WM imaginations, promoting robust actions that remain successful over the distribution of outcomes. We consider improving a behavior-cloning policy, the Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ [20], with action evaluation in WM imaginations. Specifically, we fine-tune $\pi _ { 0 . 5 ^ { - } } \mathrm { D R O I D }$ with 40 successful demonstrations per task using a weighted regression objective [13], and compare two settings: Nominal $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } ^ { \mathrm { F T } }$ , which assigns a uniform weight of 1.0 to all trajectories, and Robust $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } ^ { \mathrm { F T } }$ , which assigns weight 1.0 to trajectories that remain successful under steering toward task failures and weight 0.1 to trajectories that fail in steered imaginations. This encourages robust actions whose plausible outcome distribution does not include failures, while downweighting risky actions that can fail.

STRESSDREAM improves policies by promoting robust actions. Fig. 7 illustrates rollouts of fine-tuned $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ policies. Robust $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } ^ { \mathrm { F T } }$ favors actions whose plausible outcomes do not include task failures, as identified by steering imaginations. In contrast, Nominal $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } ^ { \mathrm { F T } }$ proposes risky actions whose plausible outcome distribution includes failures, even if they happened to succeed during data collection. Fig. 8 reports success rates over 20 rollouts per task, showing that Robust $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } ^ { \mathrm { F T } }$ achieves higher success by proposing robust actions that avoid potential failures.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we propose STRESSDREAM, which steers video world model imaginations toward high-impact outcomes specified at inference-time, while remaining within the distribution of plausible outcomes. STRESSDREAM optimizes the high-dimensional initial noise of diffusion-based video world models using a combination of semantic and plausibility objectives: VLM gradients steer generation toward high-impact outcomes specified by inference-time prompts, while typicalset constraints keep the high-dimensional noise typical under the Gaussian prior. With WMs for autonomous driving and robotic manipulation, we demonstrate that STRESSDREAM effectively steers imaginations while preserving plausibility, enabling robust policy evaluation by identifying actions with potential failures and improving policies by favoring robust actions that avoid such failures.

Limitations. STRESSDREAM requires specifying high-impact outcomes with text descriptions, so its effectiveness depends on prompt quality and may suffer from reward hacking, where the score increases without meaningful changes in generations [31, 54], highlighting the need for generalizable and robust reward models for robotics [55, 56]. In addition, STRESSDREAM may fail to preserve plausibility or to discover relevant failures when the base WM is limited: it may steer within flawed videos produced by the WM, or fail to imagine target outcomes absent from the WM’s training distribution. Thus, our notion of plausibility is limited to what the WM supports, and does not necessarily imply physical plausibility in the real world, underscoring the need for diverse robot data to train high-fidelity WMs with physically consistent predictions. Runtime also remains expensive, as current WMs take several minutes per imagination [11, 12], though this could be reduced by efficient models [57, 58, 59]. We refer readers to Appendix F for a more detailed discussion.

## A Background

## A.1 Related Works

World Models for Robotics. World models have emerged as a promising paradigm for robotics, enabling the prediction of future states directly from high-dimensional sensory observations conditioned on actions [60, 61, 62, 63, 26, 64, 65, 1, 66]. By generating synthetic trajectories, they have proven effective for vision-based policy learning [67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 17, 13], planning in latent space [26, 73], and policy evaluation [27, 12, 15]. Advances in video generation models [41], which produce realistic and temporally consistent predictions, have further increased the expressiveness of world models in diverse real-world scenarios, especially when fine-tuned with embodiment data for action conditioning [11, 74, 12]. World models typically operate under partial observability, which naturally gives rise to uncertainty and multimodality in future predictions [75, 76, 10], particularly in settings such as contact-rich manipulation [12] or autonomous driving [11, 77, 78, 74]. In particular, diffusion- and flow-matching-based [4, 5, 6] approaches have shown strong performance in modeling high-dimensional, complex environments due to their ability to capture rich, multimodal distributions over future observations [7, 8, 9, 10]. Despite this, many existing methods using world models either rely on nominal rollouts without reasoning over distribution over plausible futures [8, 12, 79] or adopt deterministic world models that cannot represent such diversity [26, 66], thereby failing to explicitly account for risks arising from uncertain futures.

Inference-time Steering of Generative Models. Inference-time steering of diffusion [4, 25] or flow-matching [6, 80] model aims to guide a pretrained generative model toward samples that maximize an inference-time reward [19, 81, 82, 83]. While directly fine-tuning diffusion models with reward objectives can be effective for reward alignment [84, 85, 86, 87, 81, 88], it is costly, complex, tied to a specific reward [19, 83], and may shift the model away from the original data distribution [42]. In contrast, inference-time steering keeps the pretrained model fixed and instead modifies the sampling procedure [89, 90, 19, 82, 91, 92], for example through classifier guidance [25] or particle-based sampling methods such as Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) [93, 94, 95, 83]. One particularly relevant direction is noise optimization, which optimizes the noise sampled at the denoising process [96, 97, 30, 31, 98, 19]. Prior work has shown that this can improve image quality [97, 99, 42], mimic the effects of diffusion guidance [43], generate rare [18, 36, 100] or diverse concepts [101], and adapt diffusion policies in robotic control [102]. However, a central challenge, especially when the noise space becomes high-dimensional, is out-of-distribution noise: samples may achieve high reward while no longer remaining in the support of the pretrained distribution once the noise deviates from the prior [18, 103, 19, 31, 104, 42, 37]. Moreover, these methods often require backpropagating reward gradients through the denoising process; in practice, many therefore rely on step-distilled models to make backpropagation tractable [105, 31, 42, 106], while recent works propose multistep score distillation to approximate reward gradients to the initial noise more efficiently [43]. Our approach adopts initial noise optimization to steer action-conditioned video world models [11, 12] with a practical solution for high-dimensional noise optimization.

## A.2 Diffusion Models

Recent generative models are often based on diffusion or flow-matching formulations, which generate samples from noise by simulating a learned differential equation. This section expands on the diffusion-model formulation in Sec. 3, which provides the foundation for recent video world models capable of generating high-quality videos. We omit conditioning on $( \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } }$ , a) throughout for brevity.

Forward Probability Path. Score-based diffusion models [4, 5, 24] and flow-matching models [107, 6] define generative models $f _ { \theta }$ whose sampling process can be described by a stochastic or ordinary differential equation (SDE or ODE). The denoising network $g _ { \theta } ^ { \tau }$ , which parameterizes these dynamics, is trained to recover the reverse generative process, typically by predicting the score of the perturbed data distribution, the injected noise, or the conditional vector field.

Starting from clean data $\mathbf { x } = \mathbf { o } \sim p ^ { \mathrm { d a t a } } ( o )$ and isotropic Gaussian noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal { N } ( 0 , \bf { I } )$ , the forward process, indexed by diffusion time $\tau \in \{ 0 , 1 , \ldots , T \}$ , defines a perturbed sample as

$$
\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau} = \alpha^ {\tau} \mathbf {x} + \sigma^ {\tau} \boldsymbol {\epsilon}.\tag{10}
$$

For variance-preserving parameterizations such as DDPM, the boundary conditions satisfy $\alpha ^ { 0 } = 1$ $\sigma ^ { 0 } = 0 .$ , and $\alpha ^ { T }  0 , \sigma ^ { T }  1$ . More generally, we only require that the terminal state be noisedominated. Representative examples include the variance-preserving schedule of DDPM [4], where $( \alpha ^ { \tau } ) ^ { 2 } + ( \sigma ^ { \tau } ) ^ { 2 } = 1$ , and the variance-exploding schedule of EDM [5], where $\alpha ^ { \tau } \equiv 1$ and $\sigma ^ { \tau }$ increases with diffusion time.

Training Objective. The denoiser $g _ { \theta } ^ { \tau }$ is trained to recover the injected Gaussian noise from a perturbed future observation, conditioned on the observation history and action sequence. With the noise-prediction parameterization,

$$
\mathcal {L} (\theta) = \mathbb {E} _ {\mathbf {x} \sim p ^ {\mathrm{data}},   \boldsymbol {\epsilon} \sim \mathcal {N} (0, \mathbf {I}),   \tau} \left[ w (\tau) \left\| \boldsymbol {\epsilon} - g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} \big (\alpha^ {\tau} \mathbf {x} + \sigma^ {\tau} \boldsymbol {\epsilon} \big) \right\| _ {2} ^ {2} \right],\tag{11}
$$

where $w ( \tau )$ is a time-dependent weighting [5]. The clean-sample, score, and velocity parameterizations are linearly equivalent [5, 25]; we take $g _ { \theta } ^ { \tau }$ as a noise predictor, and the analysis transfers to the other parameterizations.

Generation via Deterministic ODE Updates. At inference time, generation starts from an initial Gaussian noise sample $\mathbf { x } ^ { T } = \boldsymbol { \epsilon } \sim \mathcal { N } ( 0 , \mathbf { I } )$ and iteratively maps it to a clean sample by following the reverse probability-flow ODE. The deterministic update used in (3) can be viewed as a first-order Euler step of this ODE, with DDIM [24] and EDM [5] as representative instances. Given the current noisy sample $\mathbf { x } ^ { \tau }$ , the predicted clean sample is obtained by inverting the forward path (10):

$$
\hat {\mathbf {x}} ^ {0 | \tau} = \frac {\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau} - \sigma^ {\tau} g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau})}{\alpha^ {\tau}}.\tag{12}
$$

The sample is then advanced to the next, less-noisy time $\tau - 1$ by reapplying the forward path to $\hat { \mathbf { x } } ^ { 0 | \tau }$ , while using the predicted noise as the noise direction:

$$
\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau - 1} = \alpha^ {\tau - 1} \hat {\mathbf {x}} ^ {0 | \tau} + \sigma^ {\tau - 1} g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}).\tag{13}
$$

Iterating this deterministic update from $T$ to 0 defines the generative model sampling $\mathbf { x } \ \sim \ f _ { \theta }$ Substituting (12) into (13) recovers the denoising update in (3).

Probability-Flow ODE and Initial-Noise Determinism. In the continuous-time limit, the deterministic updates in (13) correspond to an Euler discretization of the probability-flow ODE associated with the forward path in $\left( 1 0 \right) [ 2 5 , 5 ]$ . This ODE transports samples from the Gaussian prior $\mathcal { N } ( 0 , \bf { I } )$ to the data distribution $p ^ { \mathrm { { d a t a } } }$ along deterministic trajectories. Therefore, the generated sample can be written as $\epsilon \mapsto f _ { \theta } ( \epsilon ) = \mathbf { x } ^ { 0 }$ , where $f _ { \theta }$ denotes the deterministic sampler induced by the trained denoiser. This makes the generated sample a deterministic and differentiable function of the initial noise, justifying the use of ϵ as a control variable and allowing gradients of downstream criteria to be backpropagated through the sampler.

## A.3 Action-conditioned Video World Models

Video world models for robotics have shown promise for policy evaluation and improvement by simulating hard-to-model systems directly from high-dimensional sensor observations [3]. In robotic manipulation, for example, a world model can anticipate how objects may spill, slip, or topple during physical interaction from camera observations [71, 79, 108]. In autonomous driving, it can simulate future scene evolution in complex environments, capturing agent interactions and safety-critical situations directly in image space [11, 74]. These capabilities can support the evaluation and improvement of visuomotor control policies in simulation, reducing reliance on costly, time-consuming, and potentially unsafe real-world trials [3, 23].

Stable Video Diffusion. Recent video world models often build on video generation models [1, 2], which have demonstrated strong ability to synthesize realistic and temporally coherent videos.

Rather than learning from scratch, many recent approaches initialize from large-scale video diffusion models and then fine-tune them on domain-specific datasets such as driving or robotic manipulation. A widely used backbone is Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) [41], a latent diffusion model for imageto-video generation. SVD uses an encoder–decoder architecture to map RGB frames into a learned latent space, $z = \mathcal { E } ( o ) \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 4 \times H / 8 \times W / 8 }$ , where H and W denote the image height and width. The diffusion model is then trained to denoise latent video representations, following an objective of the form in (11), and the generated latents are decoded back into RGB video frames using the decoder.

Action Conditioning. For robotic applications, video world models are conditioned on actions to enable controllable future generation [11, 12]. These actions may correspond to future egovehicle controls or trajectories in autonomous driving [11, 74], or to robot action sequences such as end-effector poses, joint-position commands, or low-level control commands in robotic manipulation [12, 22]. In the notation of (11), the denoiser is conditioned on histories and actions, $g _ { \theta } ^ { \tau } \left( \mathbf { x } ^ { \tau } , \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } , \mathbf { a } \right)$ , so that the generated future is constrained to be consistent with both the observed history and the specified action sequence. While earlier world models often learned latent dynamics from task-specific observation–action trajectories [67, 69, 27], recent diffusion-based video world models built on SVD typically start from a pretrained video diffusion model and post-train it with action-conditioned data. When trained with conditional dropout, these models can also use classifier-free guidance at sampling time to trade off visual realism and action controllability [109].

History Conditioning. To generate coherent future frames that remain consistent with the conditioning observations, recent video world models incorporate observation history in several ways. Some methods inject conditioning frames by replacing the corresponding noisy latent frames with clean observed latents during sampling [11]; others use progressive or autoregressive sampling with per-frame noise schedules to encourage causal structure across time [8, 74]; and some provide sparse history frames or memory tokens as additional network inputs through attention-based conditioning [12]. These mechanisms help preserve temporal continuity, keep generated futures consistent with the observed history, supporting longer-horizon, autoregressive video generation by conditioning future predictions on previous observations or generated frames.

Application: Policy Evaluation & Improvement. Action-conditioned video world models can support robot learning without repeated real-world rollouts, enabling scalable data generation, planning, policy evaluation, and policy improvement. Because current state-of-the-art video generation models are often slow and memory-intensive, they are typically used offline rather than for real-time planning. Prior work has primarily leveraged action-conditioned video prediction in two ways: (i) using imagined rollouts to evaluate or optimize policies without repeated hardware trials [15, 12, 110, 21, 23], and (ii) generating synthetic trajectories for data augmentation or policy fine-tuning [12, 111, 17, 13, 23]. In particular, recent works report that video world models can serve as simulators for closed-loop evaluation of visuomotor policies, with predicted performance correlating with real-world success rates [12]. Similarly, policies can be improved using video world models, either through reinforcement learning inside imagined rollouts [17] or by searching for successful synthetic trajectories that are then used to fine-tune the policy [13].

While our method can be applied to these broader settings, our experiments focus specifically on the value of steering video world models for robust policy evaluation and improvement. Rather than evaluating a complete robotic policy in a closed loop, we evaluate candidate trajectories that could be produced by a policy and assess their potential failure risks. Similarly, rather than improving policies through closed-loop exploration inside the world model, we use the steered world model to identify failure-prone expert trajectories and reweight them during policy fine-tuning.

## A.4 Inference-time Steering of Generative Models

Given a generative model $f _ { \theta }$ that maps an initial noise sample $\epsilon \sim \mathcal { N } ( 0 , \bf { I } )$ to a generated sample $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { x } } ~ = ~ f _ { \boldsymbol { \theta } } ( \epsilon )$ , inference-time optimization aims to find generations that maximize a user-specified criterion C, while preserving the plausibility of the generated samples with a regularizer $\operatorname { R e g } ( { \pmb x } )$

that penalizes generations that deviate from the original model distribution. This can be written as

$$
\boldsymbol {x} ^ {*} = \underset {\boldsymbol {x}} {\operatorname{argmax}} \mathcal {C} (\boldsymbol {x}) - \alpha \operatorname{Reg} (\boldsymbol {x}).\tag{14}
$$

A natural way to realize this objective is to adapt the generative model itself so that it samples from a reward-tilted distribution,

$$
f _ {\theta} ^ {*} (\boldsymbol {x}) \propto f _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {x}) \exp \left(\frac {\mathcal {C} (\boldsymbol {x})}{\alpha}\right),\tag{15}
$$

which upweights high-criterion samples while remaining close to the original model distribution. However, directly fine-tuning the generator is often impractical for inference-time control: the criterion may change across tasks or users, and fine-tuning requires significant computation for each new criterion. Moreover, preserving closeness to the original distribution can require density estimates that are intractable or prohibitively expensive for high-dimensional generative models [85, 112]. As a result, model adaptation can drift away from the original data distribution and produce implausible generations [42, 83].

Initial Noise Optimization. Initial noise optimization instead keeps the generative model fixed and optimizes only the input noise according to an inference-time criterion evaluated on the generated sample:

$$
\epsilon^ {*} = \underset {\epsilon} {\operatorname{argmax}}   \mathcal {C} (f _ {\theta} (\epsilon)).\tag{16}
$$

This formulation allows arbitrary criteria to be specified at inference time without modifying the model parameters. If the optimized noise remains plausible under the original Gaussian prior, the resulting generation is still induced by the pretrained model distribution, so additional regularization on the generated sample itself is not required.

When $\mathcal { C }$ is differentiable, the optimization can be solved by backpropagating through the diffusion sampler [96, 30, 31]. For non-differentiable criteria, prior work estimates gradients of the criterion [19], amortizes the optimization with a hypernetwork [42], or formulates steering as a reinforcement learning problem [99, 102]. Since backpropagating through many iterative denoising steps is computationally expensive, several methods further distill diffusion models into one-step generators to make optimization more tractable [31, 105, 42].

## A.5 Typical Set of High-Dimensional Gaussian Noise

The typical set describes where samples from a distribution are expected to lie with high probability. It is therefore a notion of probability mass, rather than simply probability density [33, 35]. For a random vector $\epsilon \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ , let $p ( \epsilon )$ denote the joint density of the full D-dimensional Gaussian prior. The δ-typical set can be written as

$$
\mathcal {T} _ {\delta} ^ {(D)} = \left\{\epsilon : \left| - \frac {1}{D} \log p (\epsilon) - h (\mathcal {N} (0, 1)) \right| \leq \delta \right\},\tag{17}
$$

where $\begin{array} { r } { h ( \mathcal { N } ( 0 , 1 ) ) = \frac { 1 } { 2 } \log ( 2 \pi e ) } \end{array}$ is the differential entropy of a one-dimensional standard Gaussian. The one-dimensional entropy appears because the standard Gaussian prior factorizes across coordinates, $\begin{array} { r } { p ( \epsilon ) = \prod _ { i = 1 } ^ { D } p ( \epsilon _ { i } ) } \end{array}$ . Thu $\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { s } , - \frac { 1 } { D } \log p ( \epsilon ) } \end{array}$ ) is the average information content per coordinate, analogous to the empirical entropy of an i.i.d. sequence. For the isotropic Gaussian prior,

$$
- \frac {1}{D} \log p (\epsilon) = \frac {1}{2} \log (2 \pi) + \frac {1}{2 D} \| \epsilon \| _ {2} ^ {2}.\tag{18}
$$

Therefore, typicality implies $\| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } / D \approx 1$ . In high dimensions, Gaussian samples concentrate on a thin shell of radius approximately ${ \sqrt { D } } .$ , rather than near the mode. This illustrates the key distinction between density and typicality: the zero vector has the highest density under the Gaussian prior, but is extremely unlikely to be sampled in high dimensions. Although the Gaussian distribution has full support over $\mathbb { R } ^ { D }$ , most of its probability mass concentrates on a small subset of that space as D grows. Equivalently, a typical sample is not characterized only by its likelihood, but by a collection of sample statistics that take values close to their expectations under the Gaussian prior.

## B Inference-time Steering of Video World Models

## B.1 Regularizing Noise within the Typical Set of High-Dimensional Gaussian Distribution

In this section, we elaborate on Sec. 4.2, where we impose typical-set constraints during noise optimization. In high-dimensional Gaussian distributions, regions of high probability density do not necessarily coincide with regions of high probability mass, where typical samples concentrate. For example, the zero vector has the highest density under a standard Gaussian, yet it is almost never sampled in high dimensions; instead, typical samples lie on a thin shell with squared norm approximately d. We refer to this high-mass region as the typical set. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the typical set is distinct from the high-density region, and sampling noise outside the typical set can lead to implausible generations. This distinction is important for diffusion models, which are trained to denoise samples initialized from typical Gaussian noise. If optimization pushes the initial noise toward atypical, low-mass regions of the prior, the denoiser may be evaluated on inputs rarely seen during training, leading to approximation errors and, consequently, implausible or OOD generations.

## B.2 Concentration of Gaussian Typical Set Regularizers

Norm Concentration. For Gaussian noise $\mathbf { \epsilon } \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ , the squared norm satisfies $\| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } \sim \chi _ { D } ^ { 2 }$ and therefore concentrates sharply around D. Equivalently, the Euclidean norm, or radius $\lVert \epsilon \rVert _ { 2 }$ concentrates around $\sqrt { D }$ . Moreover, if $R = \| \epsilon \| _ { 2 }$ , then R follows a chi distribution with D degrees of freedom, whose standard deviation remains bounded independently of D and approaches $1 / \sqrt { 2 }$ as $D \to \infty$ . Thus, although the typical radius grows as $\sqrt { D } .$ the absolute width of the shell containing most Gaussian probability mass remains approximately constant. This motivates penalizing deviations from the typical Gaussian shell in radius:

$$
\mathcal {C} ^ {\text { norm }} (\epsilon) := - \left(\| \epsilon \| _ {2} - \sqrt {D}\right) ^ {2}.
$$

Lemma 1 (Norm concentration of Gaussian noise). Let $\epsilon \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ . Then, for any $\delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(\left| \frac {\| \boldsymbol {\epsilon} \| _ {2} ^ {2}}{D} - 1 \right| > 2 \sqrt {\frac {\log (2 / \delta)}{D}} + 2 \frac {\log (2 / \delta)}{D}\right) \leq \delta .
$$

Equivalently, the probability that ϵ lies outside this typical norm shell is at most $\delta .$

Proof. Since $\epsilon \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ , its coordinates $\epsilon _ { 1 } , \ldots , \epsilon _ { D }$ are independent standard normal random variables. Hence,

$$
\| \boldsymbol {\epsilon} \| _ {2} ^ {2} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {D} \epsilon_ {j} ^ {2} \sim \chi_ {D} ^ {2}.
$$

Let $X = \| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 }$ . By the Laurent–Massart inequality [113], for any $x > 0 .$

$$
\mathbb {P} \Big (X - D \geq 2 \sqrt {D x} + 2 x \Big) \leq e ^ {- x}, \quad \mathbb {P} \Big (D - X \geq 2 \sqrt {D x} \Big) \leq e ^ {- x}.
$$

Taking $x = \log ( 2 / \delta )$ , each tail event has probability at most $\delta / 2 .$ . Therefore, by a union bound, with probability at least $1 - \delta .$

$$
- 2 \sqrt {D \log (2 / \delta)} \leq X - D \leq 2 \sqrt {D \log (2 / \delta)} + 2 \log (2 / \delta).
$$

Dividing by D gives

$$
\left| \frac {X}{D} - 1 \right| \leq 2 \sqrt {\frac {\log (2 / \delta)}{D}} + 2 \frac {\log (2 / \delta)}{D}.
$$

Substituting $X = \| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 }$ completes the proof.

Corollary 1 (High-probability scale of the radial norm regularizer). For

$$
\left| \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { norm }} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) \right| = \left(\left\| \boldsymbol {\epsilon} \right\| _ {2} - \sqrt {D}\right) ^ {2},
$$

we have, for any $\delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ , with probability at least $1 - \delta ,$

$$
\left| \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { norm }} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) \right| \leq \left(2 \sqrt {\log (2 / \delta)} + 2 \frac {\log (2 / \delta)}{\sqrt {D}}\right) ^ {2}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\left| \mathcal {C} ^ {\text { norm }} (\epsilon) \right| = O \left(\log (1 / \delta) + \frac {\log^ {2} (1 / \delta)}{D}\right)
$$

with high probability.

Proof. By Lemma 1, with probability at least $1 - \delta ,$

$$
\left| \| \epsilon \| _ {2} ^ {2} - D \right| \leq 2 \sqrt {D \log (2 / \delta)} + 2 \log (2 / \delta).
$$

Let $R = \| \epsilon \| _ { 2 }$ . Since

$$
| R - \sqrt {D} | = \frac {| R ^ {2} - D |}{R + \sqrt {D}} \leq \frac {| R ^ {2} - D |}{\sqrt {D}},
$$

we obtain

$$
| R - \sqrt {D} | \leq 2 \sqrt {\log (2 / \delta)} + 2 \frac {\log (2 / \delta)}{\sqrt {D}}.
$$

Squaring both sides gives the desired bound.

Corollary 2 (Dimension-independent width of the Gaussian shell). Let $\begin{array} { r l r } { R } & { { } = } & { \| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } } \end{array}$ with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ . Then the standard deviation of R is bounded independently of D:

$$
\operatorname{std} (R) = O (1).
$$

More specifically, R follows a chi distribution with D degrees of freedom, and

$$
\operatorname{std} (R) \to \frac {1}{\sqrt {2}} \qquad a s \qquad D \to \infty .
$$

Proof. Since $R = \| \epsilon \| _ { 2 }$ and $\| \epsilon \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } \sim \chi _ { D } ^ { 2 }$ , the random variable R follows a chi distribution with D degrees of freedom. The chi distribution has moments

$$
\mathbb {E} [ R ^ {2} ] = D, \qquad \mathbb {E} [ R ] = \sqrt {2} \frac {\Gamma ((D + 1) / 2)}{\Gamma (D / 2)}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{Var} (R) = D - 2 \left(\frac {\Gamma ((D + 1) / 2)}{\Gamma (D / 2)}\right) ^ {2}.
$$

This variance remains bounded as $D$ grows, and in fact converges to $1 / 2$ . Hence

$$
\mathrm{std} (R) \to \frac {1}{\sqrt {2}},
$$

showing that the width of the Gaussian shell is asymptotically independent of the dimension.

Isotropy Concentration. We now show that the isotropy regularizer is small with high probability under the Gaussian prior. Let $\epsilon \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ , and suppose $D = m k$ . We randomly permute the coordinates of ϵ and partition them into m blocks

$$
\epsilon_ {1}, \ldots , \epsilon_ {m} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {k}.
$$

Since the Gaussian prior has i.i.d. coordinates, this random permutation does not change the distribution, and the blocks satisfy

$$
\epsilon_ {i} \stackrel {\mathrm{i.i.d.}} {\sim} \mathcal {N} (\mathbf {0}, \mathbf {I} _ {k}).
$$

Thus, their empirical second moment

$$
\widehat {\boldsymbol {\Sigma}} := \frac {1}{m} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {i} \boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {i} ^ {\top}
$$

should be close to $\mathbf { I } _ { k }$ under the Gaussian prior. We measure deviations from this blockwise isotropy using

$$
\mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{iso}} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) := - \frac {1}{k} \left\| \widehat {\boldsymbol {\Sigma}} - \mathbf {I} _ {k} \right\| _ {F} ^ {2}.
$$

Lemma 2 (Small probability of anisotropic Gaussian block covariance). There exists a universal constant $C > 0$ such that, for any $\delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ ), if m $\gtrsim \log ( 2 k ^ { 2 } / \delta )$ , then

$$
\mathbb {P} \bigg (| \mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{iso}} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) | > C \frac {k \log (2 k ^ {2} / \delta)}{m} \bigg) \leq \delta .
$$

Proof. For each entry of $\widehat { \pmb { \Sigma } }$ ,

$$
\widehat {\Sigma} _ {a b} = \frac {1}{m} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \epsilon_ {i, a} \epsilon_ {i, b}.
$$

First, consider the diagonal terms. Since $\epsilon _ { i , a } \sim \mathcal { N } ( 0 , 1 )$ 2

$$
\widehat {\Sigma} _ {a a} = \frac {1}{m} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \epsilon_ {i, a} ^ {2}, \quad \text { therefore }, \quad \mathbb {E} [ \widehat {\Sigma} _ {a a} ] = 1, \quad \operatorname{Var} (\widehat {\Sigma} _ {a a}) = \frac {2}{m}, \quad \Rightarrow \mathbb {E} \left[ (\widehat {\Sigma} _ {a a} - 1) ^ {2} \right] = \frac {2}{m}.
$$

For the off-diagonal terms a $\neq b ,$ , the products $\epsilon _ { i , a } \epsilon _ { i , b }$ have mean zero and variance one. Hence

$$
\mathbb {E} [ \widehat {\Sigma} _ {a b} ] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var} (\widehat {\Sigma} _ {a b}) = \frac {1}{m}, \quad \text {   which   gives   } \quad \mathbb {E} \left[ \widehat {\Sigma} _ {a b} ^ {2} \right] = \frac {1}{m}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathbb {E} \left[ \left\| \widehat {\boldsymbol {\Sigma}} - \mathbf {I} _ {k} \right\| _ {F} ^ {2} \right] = \sum_ {a = 1} ^ {k} \mathbb {E} \left[ (\widehat {\Sigma} _ {a a} - 1) ^ {2} \right] + \sum_ {a \neq b} \mathbb {E} \left[ \widehat {\Sigma} _ {a b} ^ {2} \right]} \\ & {\qquad = k \cdot \frac {2}{m} + k (k - 1) \cdot \frac {1}{m}} \\ & {\qquad = \frac {k ^ {2} + k}{m}.} \end{array}
$$

Dividing by k gives

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ | \mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{iso}} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) | \right] = \frac {k + 1}{m}.
$$

For the high-probability statement, note that each diagonal error $\widehat { \Sigma } _ { a a } - 1$ is an average of centered sub-exponential random variables, and each off-diagonal entry $\widehat { \Sigma } _ { a b }$ is an average of products of independent Gaussians, which are also sub-exponential. Therefore, by Bernstein-type concentration [114], for all entries,

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(| \widehat {\Sigma} _ {a b} - \delta_ {a b} | > t\right) \leq 2 \exp (- c m t ^ {2})
$$

for $0 < t \lesssim 1$ , where $c > 0$ is a universal constant. Applying a union bound over the $k ^ { 2 }$ entries and taking

$$
t = C ^ {\prime} \sqrt {\frac {\log (2 k ^ {2} / \delta)}{m}},
$$

we obtain, with probability at least $1 - \delta .$

$$
| \widehat {\Sigma} _ {a b} - \delta_ {a b} | \leq C ^ {\prime} \sqrt {\frac {\log (2 k ^ {2} / \delta)}{m}} \quad \text {   for   all   } a, b.
$$

Hence,

$$
\left\| \widehat {\boldsymbol {\Sigma}} - \mathbf {I} _ {k} \right\| _ {F} ^ {2} \leq k ^ {2} C ^ {\prime 2} \frac {\log (2 k ^ {2} / \delta)}{m}.
$$

Dividing by k yields

$$
\mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{iso}} (\epsilon) \leq C \frac {k \log (2 k ^ {2} / \delta)}{m}
$$

with probability at least $1 - \delta$ , for a universal constant $C > 0$

This shows that the normalized isotropy penalty is small under the training-time Gaussian prior whenever $m \gg k$ . Unlike norm concentration, which only constrains the total energy $\operatorname { t r } ( { \hat { \Sigma } } )$ ), the isotropy penalty also discourages unequal coordinate variances and nonzero off-diagonal correlations. Therefore, it detects structured coordinate patterns that may still have a typical global norm but are unlikely under an i.i.d. Gaussian prior.

Spectral-Whiteness Concentration. The isotropy regularizer checks whether randomly grouped coordinates have approximately identity covariance, but it does not directly rule out spatial frequency structure. For example, optimized noise may have excessive low-frequency energy, producing smooth spatial patterns, or excessive high-frequency energy, producing oscillatory artifacts. Under an i.i.d. Gaussian prior, the noise should instead be spectrally white: its energy should be spread evenly across spatial frequencies.

To formalize this, consider one spatial slice of the noise and let

$$
\widetilde {\epsilon} = U \epsilon
$$

be its discrete Fourier transform, where U is an orthonormal Fourier basis. For real-valued noise, this can be interpreted as the equivalent real sine–cosine basis. Since the standard Gaussian distribution is rotationally invariant,

$$
\widetilde {\epsilon} \sim \mathcal {N} (\mathbf {0}, \mathbf {I}).
$$

Thus, no frequency band should systematically contain more energy than another. Individual Fourier powers $| \widetilde { \epsilon } _ { j } | ^ { 2 }$ are noisy, but averages over sufficiently large frequency bins concentrate.

Let $\{ B _ { b } \} _ { b = 1 } ^ { B }$ be disjoint frequency bins, and define the average power in each bin by

$$
\widehat {p} _ {b} := \frac {1}{| \mathcal {B} _ {b} |} \sum_ {j \in \mathcal {B} _ {b}} | \widetilde {\epsilon} _ {j} | ^ {2}.
$$

We penalize deviations from a flat binned spectrum using

$$
\mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{spec}} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) := - \frac {1}{B} \sum_ {b = 1} ^ {B} \left(\widehat {p} _ {b} - \bar {p}\right) ^ {2}, \quad \bar {p} := \frac {1}{B} \sum_ {b = 1} ^ {B} \widehat {p} _ {b}.
$$

Lemma 3 (Small probability of atypical spectral energy). Let $\mathbf { \epsilon } \gets \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } )$ , and let $\widetilde { \epsilon } = U \epsilon$ , where U is an orthonormal discrete Fourier transform. Let $\{ B _ { b } \} _ { b = 1 } ^ { B }$ be disjoint frequency bins, and define $\mathcal { C } ^ { \mathrm { s p e c } }$ as above. Let

$$
n _ {\min} := \min _ {b} | \mathcal {B} _ {b} |.
$$

Then, for any $\delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$

$$
\mathbb {P} \left(| \mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{spec}} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) | > 4 \left(2 \sqrt {\frac {\log (2 B / \delta)}{n _ {\min}}} + 2 \frac {\log (2 B / \delta)}{n _ {\min}}\right) ^ {2}\right) \leq \delta .
$$

Proof. Since U is orthonormal and the standard Gaussian distribution is rotationally invariant,

$$
\widetilde {\epsilon} = U \epsilon \sim \mathcal {N} (\mathbf {0}, \mathbf {I}).
$$

Thus, in the equivalent real orthonormal Fourier representation, the Fourier coefficients are independent standard normal random variables.

For each frequency bin $B _ { b }$ ,

$$
| \mathcal {B} _ {b} | \widehat {p _ {b}} = \sum_ {j \in \mathcal {B} _ {b}} | \widetilde {\epsilon} _ {j} | ^ {2} \sim \chi_ {| \mathcal {B} _ {b} |} ^ {2}.
$$

By the Laurent–Massart inequality [113] and a union bound over the B bins, with probability at least $1 - \delta .$ , for all $b = 1 , \dots , B$

$$
| \widehat {p} _ {b} - 1 | \leq 2 \sqrt {\frac {\log (2 B / \delta)}{| \mathcal {B} _ {b} |}} + 2 \frac {\log (2 B / \delta)}{| \mathcal {B} _ {b} |}.
$$

Since $\left. B _ { b } \right. \ge n _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } .$ , we have

$$
\max _ {b} | \widehat {p} _ {b} - 1 | \leq \eta , \quad \eta := 2 \sqrt {\frac {\log (2 B / \delta)}{n _ {\min}}} + 2 \frac {\log (2 B / \delta)}{n _ {\min}}.
$$

Moreover,

$$
| \bar {p} - 1 | = \left| \frac {1}{B} \sum_ {b = 1} ^ {B} (\widehat {p} _ {b} - 1) \right| \leq \eta .
$$

Therefore, for every bin,

$$
| \widehat {p} _ {b} - \bar {p} | \leq | \widehat {p} _ {b} - 1 | + | \bar {p} - 1 | \leq 2 \eta .
$$

Substituting this into the definition of the regularizer gives

$$
\left| \mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{spec}} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) \right| = \frac {1}{B} \sum_ {b = 1} ^ {B} \left(\widehat {p} _ {b} - \bar {p}\right) ^ {2} \leq 4 \eta^ {2}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\mathbb {P} \big (| \mathcal {C} ^ {\mathrm{spec}} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}) | > 4 \eta^ {2} \big) \leq \delta ,
$$

which proves the claim.

Lemma 3 shows that the spectral-whiteness penalty is small under the Gaussian prior when each frequency bin contains sufficiently many coefficients. Thus, a large value indicates an atypical spatial frequency structure. This regularizer is complementary to isotropy: isotropy checks covariance after random coordinate grouping, whereas spectral whiteness checks whether energy is evenly distributed across spatial frequencies.

## B.4 Score-Distillation for Approximating Initial-Noise Gradients

This section justifies the gradient approximation used in (8). We omit the conditioning variables $( \mathbf { o } ^ { \mathrm { h i s t } } , \mathbf { a } )$ for readability. Let the deterministic sampler define the reverse trajectory

$$
\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau - 1} = d ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}), \qquad \mathbf {x} ^ {T} = \boldsymbol {\epsilon}, \qquad \mathbf {x} ^ {0} = f _ {\theta} (\boldsymbol {\epsilon}),
$$

where each denoising step follows the update in (3):

$$
d ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}) = \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \left(\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau} - \sigma^ {\tau} g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau})\right) + \sigma^ {\tau - 1} g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}).
$$

For a differentiable criterion $\mathcal { C } ( \mathbf { x } ^ { 0 } )$ , the exact initial-noise gradient is obtained by the chain rule:

$$
\nabla_ {\boldsymbol {\epsilon}} \mathcal {C} (\mathbf {x} ^ {0}) = \left[ \prod_ {\tau = T} ^ {1} \frac {\partial d ^ {\tau}}{\partial \mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}} \right] ^ {\top} \nabla_ {\mathbf {x} ^ {0}} \mathcal {C} (\mathbf {x} ^ {0}),
$$

where the product is ordered along the reverse denoising trajectory. The Jacobian of each step is

$$
\frac {\partial d ^ {\tau}}{\partial \mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}} = \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \mathbf {I} + \left(\sigma^ {\tau - 1} - \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \sigma^ {\tau}\right) \mathbf {J} _ {g} ^ {\tau}, \text {   where   } \mathbf {J} _ {g} ^ {\tau} = \frac {\partial g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau})}{\partial \mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}}.
$$

Thus, exact backpropagation requires multiplying by all denoiser Jacobians and storing the full denoising computation graph.

Proportional-Jacobian approximation. Ahn et al. [43] empirically observe that the denoiser Jacobian is approximately diagonal and often behaves like a timestep-dependent multiple of the identity. Under the idealized approximation

$$
\mathbf {J} _ {g} ^ {\tau} \approx \rho^ {\tau} \mathbf {I},
$$

the sampler Jacobian becomes

$$
\prod_ {\tau = T} ^ {1} \left[ \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \mathbf {I} + \left(\sigma^ {\tau - 1} - \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \sigma^ {\tau}\right) \mathbf {J} _ {g} ^ {\tau} \right] \approx \beta \mathbf {I}, \text {   where   } \beta := \prod_ {\tau = T} ^ {1} \left[ \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} + \left(\sigma^ {\tau - 1} - \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \sigma^ {\tau}\right) \rho^ {\tau} \right].
$$

Substituting this into the exact chain rule yields

$$
\nabla_ {\epsilon} \mathcal {C} (f _ {\theta} (\epsilon)) \approx \beta \nabla_ {\mathbf {x} ^ {0}} \mathcal {C} (\mathbf {x} ^ {0}), \qquad \mathbf {x} ^ {0} = f _ {\theta} (\epsilon).
$$

This is the approximation used in the main text: instead of differentiating through the sampler, we compute the criterion gradient with respect to the generated clean latent and use it as a scaled proxy for the initial-noise gradient.

Diagonal-Jacobian variant. If the denoiser Jacobian is diagonal but not exactly proportional to the identity,

$$
\mathbf {J} _ {g} ^ {\tau} \approx \mathrm{diag} (\pmb {\rho} ^ {\tau}),
$$

then the same derivation gives a diagonal preconditioner

$$
\nabla_ {\epsilon} \mathcal {C} (\mathbf {x} ^ {0}) \approx \mathbf {B} \nabla_ {\mathbf {x} ^ {0}} \mathcal {C} (\mathbf {x} ^ {0}),
$$

where

$$
\mathbf {B} = \prod_ {\tau = T} ^ {1} \operatorname{diag} \left[ \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \mathbf {1} + \left(\sigma^ {\tau - 1} - \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \sigma^ {\tau}\right) \boldsymbol {\rho} ^ {\tau} \right].
$$

The scalar approximation $\mathbf { B } \approx \beta \mathbf { I }$ is therefore a practical simplification: it assumes the diagonal entries vary slowly enough that their average effect can be absorbed into the optimization step size.

Connection to stop-gradient MSD. Multistep score distillation [43] keeps the forward denoising trajectory unchanged but applies a stop-gradient operator to the denoiser outputs during backpropagation. In our notation, the stop-gradient version of one denoising step is

$$
d _ {\mathrm{SG}} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}) = \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \left(\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau} - \sigma^ {\tau} \operatorname{SG} [ g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}) ]\right) + \sigma^ {\tau - 1} \operatorname{SG} [ g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}) ],
$$

where $\mathrm { S G } [ \cdot ]$ is the identity in the forward pass and has zero derivative in the backward pass. Therefore, this removes $\mathbf { J } _ { g } ^ { \tau }$ from the backward pass, leaving only the explicit linear dependence on $\mathbf { x } ^ { \tau } \cdot$ :

$$
\frac {\partial d _ {\mathrm{SG}} ^ {\tau}}{\partial \mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}} = \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \mathbf {I}.
$$

Under the same proportional-Jacobian approximation, both the full-gradient and stop-gradient Jacobian products are scalar multiples of the identity:

$$
\prod_ {\tau = T} ^ {1} \frac {\partial d ^ {\tau}}{\partial \mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}} \approx \beta \mathbf {I}, \qquad \prod_ {\tau = T} ^ {1} \frac {\partial d _ {\mathrm{SG}} ^ {\tau}}{\partial \mathbf {x} ^ {\tau}} = \left(\prod_ {\tau = T} ^ {1} \frac {\alpha^ {\tau - 1}}{\alpha^ {\tau}}\right) \mathbf {I}.
$$

Therefore, the stop-gradient gradient and the full sampler gradient differ primarily by timestepdependent scalar factors. Our approximation uses this same principle, but applies it directly to optimize the initial noise: the clean-latent criterion gradient is used as a proxy direction for the initial-noise gradient, with the unknown scalar absorbed into $\beta$ and the learning rate.

Efficacy of Gradient Approximation for Noise Optimization. The noise-gradient approximation enables efficient gradient-based steering without backpropagating through the iterative denoising process of large-scale video diffusion models. However, it relies on the assumption that the Jacobian of the diffusion sampler is approximately diagonal [43]. Indeed, prior work on noise optimization for text-to-image generation often relies on onestep distilled generators to make noise optimization tractable without differentiating through the full iterative sampling process.

## B.5 Implementation Details

We summarize the overall procedure of STRESSDREAM in Algorithm 1. To improve optimization stability, we use both global gradient-norm clipping and per-coordinate gradient clipping. We additionally monitor the Euclidean norm of the noise and project it back to the typical shell, with norm ${ \sqrt { D } } ,$ , whenever it deviates by more than 3.0. This threshold is conservative: the standard deviation of the Gaussian norm is approximately 0.707 in high dimensions, as shown in Corollary 2, so the interval contains more than 99.99% of typical Gaussian samples. In addition, we omit momentum in the optimizer, since accumulated updates can easily push the noise outside the typical set.

Baseline: Classifier Guidance. We also compare against classifier guidance [44, 45], which modifies the denoising process at each diffusion step using the gradient of the failure criterion. In the world-model setting, however, such guidance is difficult to apply directly. The failure predictor $\ell _ { \theta }$ is trained on clean latent states, not on noisy intermediate diffusion states. Therefore, evaluating $\ell _ { \theta }$ directly on $z _ { t } ^ { \tau }$ can provide unreliable gradients, while exact guidance would require backpropagating through the denoiser at every denoising step.

To make classifier guidance tractable, we use a one-step clean-latent approximation [45]. Given a noisy latent $z _ { t + 1 } ^ { \tau }$ , we estimate the corresponding clean latent by inverting the forward diffusion path:

$$
\hat {z} _ {t + 1} ^ {0 | \tau} = \frac {z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau} - \sigma^ {\tau} g _ {\theta} ^ {\tau} (z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau} , z _ {t} , a _ {t})}{\alpha^ {\tau}}.
$$

The guidance gradient is then computed on this approximately clean latent:

$$
\nabla_ {z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau}} \ell_ {\theta} (\hat {z} _ {t + 1} ^ {0 | \tau}) \approx \frac {1}{\alpha^ {\tau}} \nabla_ {\hat {z} _ {t + 1} ^ {0 | \tau}} \ell_ {\theta} (\hat {z} _ {t + 1} ^ {0 | \tau}),
$$

which avoids backpropagating through the denoising network, but it can introduce bias because the one-step clean prediction may be misaligned with the final generated latent after the full denoising trajectory. This issue is particularly pronounced in action-conditioned world models, where small guidance errors can push the generation toward high-failure latents that are inconsistent with the conditioning action sequence or the learned dynamics.

Empirical Density Estimation. We additionally evaluate whether the generated samples themselves remain in-distribution by fitting a flow-matching density surrogate in the latent observation space [121]. Let $z = \mathcal { E } _ { \theta } ( o )$ ) denote the latent representation of an observation from the training dataset. We train a flow-matching vector field $q _ { \psi }$ that transports training latents to standard Gaussian noise. Specifically, for $w \sim \mathcal { N } ( 0 , \mathbf { I } )$ and $\tau \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , define the linear interpolation

$$
z ^ {\tau} = z + \tau (w - z).
$$

The vector field is trained to predict the displacement toward the Gaussian endpoint:

$$
q _ {\psi} (z ^ {\tau}, \tau) \approx w - z.
$$

Given a generated latent $\hat { z } _ { t }$ , we estimate its corresponding Gaussian endpoint with a one-step prediction from $\tau = 0 \colon$

$$
\widehat {w} _ {t} = \widehat {z} _ {t} + q _ {\psi} (\widehat {z} _ {t}, 0).
$$

If $\hat { z } _ { t }$ is in-distribution, then $\widehat { w } _ { t }$ should be close to a sample from $\mathcal { N } ( 0 , \bf { I } )$ . Since the Gaussian logdensity is proportional $\mathrm { t o } - \| \widehat { \boldsymbol { w } } _ { t } \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 }$ , we use

$$
s _ {\mathrm{OOD}} (\hat {z} _ {t}) := \| \widehat {w} _ {t} \| _ {2} ^ {2}
$$

as the latent OOD score. A higher score indicates that the generated latent is farther from the distribution of encoded in-distribution observations. This density-based OOD score complements the noise-space regularizers by checking whether the resulting generated samples, not only the optimized initial noise, remain plausible.

## D.2 Robotic Manipulation: Task Details

We describe the manipulation task setups, the criteria used to determine success or failure, and the language prompts used for steering the generated futures. Visual examples of the task setups are shown in Fig. 7 and on the project website.

Block Stack. Two Jenga blocks, one orange and one blue, are placed on the table. The robot is tasked with stacking the orange block on top of the blue block. A trajectory is considered a success if the orange block remains on top of the blue block, and a failure if the robot fails to stack the block or if the orange block falls off after stacking. VLM prompt: Are the two blocks stacked? Respond with a single word: Yes or No.

Knife Put. A lightweight plastic bowl and a knife are placed on the table. The robot is tasked with picking up the knife and placing it inside the bowl without causing either object to fall. This task is challenging because the knife has an uneven center of mass and a complex shape, while the lightweight bowl can easily tip over. A trajectory is considered a success if the knife is placed inside the bowl and remains there, and a failure if the knife falls out or the bowl tips over. VLM prompt: Is the knife placed inside of the blue bowl without falling out? Respond with a single word: Yes or No.

Stacked Utensil Pick. A blue knife and a red spoon are overlapped on a green plate, with the red spoon placed on top of the blue knife. The robot is instructed to pick up the blue knife while keeping the red spoon on the plate. A trajectory is considered a success if the blue knife is successfully picked up and the spoon remains on the plate, and a failure if the blue knife is not picked up or if the spoon falls off the plate. The robot should therefore remove the lower utensil while safely leaving the upper utensil on the plate. VLM prompt: Is the red spoon in the green plate? Respond with a single word: Yes or No.

Coffee Bean Pour. A bowl is on top of a coffee scale, and a cup contains coffee beans. The robot is tasked with pouring the coffee beans from the cup into the bowl on the scale. A trajectory is considered a success if the coffee beans are poured into the bowl without spilling, and a failure if any coffee beans spill onto the table. VLM prompt: Is the coffee bean spilled on the table? Respond with a single word: Yes or No.

Open Coffee Bag. An open coffee bag with a variable and partially unobservable amount of coffee beans inside is on the table, along with a container. The robot is instructed to place the coffee bag inside the container. A trajectory is considered a failure if any coffee beans spill out of the bag, either into the container or onto the table. Thus, the robot must avoid spilling while lifting, carrying, and placing the bag into the container. VLM prompt: Is the coffee bean spilled? Respond with a single word: Yes or No.

Open Candy Bag. An open gummy bear candy bag is on the table, and the robot is instructed to place it inside the container without spilling. Compared to coffee beans, gummy bears are stickier and heavier, creating a smaller risk of spilling out of the bag. A trajectory is considered a success if the gummy bears remain inside the bag, and a failure if any gummy bears spill out. VLM prompt: Does candy spill from an open bag? Respond with a single word: Yes or No.

## D.3 Autonomous Driving: Evaluation Data Curation

To evaluate steering for autonomous driving video world models, we curate an evaluation set from the PAI-AV dataset [46]. We focus on scenarios in which the future evolution is uncertain or interaction-dependent, such as pedestrian crossing, vehicle merging, traffic-light changes, and stopsign behavior. Table 8 summarizes the event categories and the verifier prompts used for Qwen-VL [32] and X-CLIP [50]. For X-CLIP, the first text description is treated as the positive event description, while the remaining descriptions are used as negative alternatives.

To retrieve candidate clips from the large-scale dataset, we first extract VLM reasoning traces from Alpamayo-R1 [123] on PAI-AV clips. We then embed these traces using Sentence-BERT [124] and retrieve the top-k clips whose embeddings are most similar to text descriptions of each event category. Human annotators then verify whether the target event occurs in each retrieved clip and label the event occurrence time. Since the video world model generates 2.5s futures, we select the frame 2.5s before the annotated event time as the initial observation for evaluation. We similarly curate an evaluation set of 200 collision clips from the Nexar Collision Prediction Dataset [47] by randomly sampling from the provided collision annotations.

## D.4 Evaluation Metrics

Autonomous Driving. To evaluate steering, we use held-out metrics that are not used during optimization. This is important because a steered generation can increase the optimization score without producing a meaningful or plausible change in the video. We therefore evaluate two complementary aspects: (i) target alignment, i.e., whether the generated video clearly depicts the target event described by the text prompt, and (ii) plausibility, i.e., whether the generated video remains physically reasonable and free from severe visual artifacts that indicate out-of-distribution generation.

For autonomous driving, we adopt WorldModelBench (WMB) [53], which evaluates video worldmodel generations using both text-prompt alignment and video-quality criteria. We use WMB because it includes driving data in its evaluation distribution and, empirically, provides meaningful scores for whether generated driving videos both match the target prompt and remain visually plausible. WMB reports three scores: instruction following (0–3), physics adherence (0–5), and commonsense consistency (0–2). We use the instruction-following score to measure target alignment, and use physics adherence and commonsense consistency as proxies for generation plausibility.

In addition, we report a target-alignment score from an external VLM judge. This judge is used only for evaluation and is not used during steering. We use the following prompt template with Gemini [117] to obtain a 0–10 alignment score:

```txt
You are an expert evaluator of autonomous driving world models. Your ONLY task is to judge how well the video follows the text instruction. TEXT DESCRIPTION: {prompt}

SCORING RUBRIC (0-10):

10: The video is aligned with the text description clearly and completely.

7-9: The video is aligned with the text description but with minor deviations (e.g., slightly wrong direction or timing).

4-6: The video is vaguely related to the text description, but the specific action does not happen.

1-3: The video is completely unrelated to the text description.

0: The video content is completely unrelated to the text description.
```

## F Limitations & Discussions

Suboptimality of Noise Optimization. While STRESSDREAM provides a practical instantiation of steering video WM imaginations via gradient-based noise optimization, the optimized noise may still be suboptimal. The optimization in STRESSDREAM should be viewed as a local refinement of the initial noise rather than a global search over all possible futures. Exhaustively searching the high-dimensional noise space is infeasible: if $\epsilon , \epsilon ^ { \prime } \stackrel { \mathrm { i . i . d . } } { \sim } \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ , then <sub>√</sub> $\epsilon - \epsilon ^ { \prime } \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , 2 \mathbf { I } _ { D } )$ , so $\| \epsilon - \epsilon ^ { \prime } \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } / 2 \sim \chi _ { D } ^ { 2 }$ , and therefore $\| \epsilon - \epsilon ^ { \prime } \| _ { 2 }$ concentrates around $\sqrt { 2 D }$ . In contrast, the average $L _ { 2 }$ distance between the initial and optimized noises in our experiments is far smaller, indicating that STRESSDREAM performs local refinement rather than global exploration. The typical-set constraints make gradient updates reliable mainly within a moderate local region; overly large updates can push the noise OOD and make generations implausible. A natural extension is to combine Bestof-N-style search with gradient-based refinement [131, 92], or to learn a value function [83] to guide denoising. Finally, for video WMs that require autoregressive rollouts, later generations condition on previously generated futures. The optimal solution would therefore require jointly optimizing a sequence of noises across rollout segments, but the current implementation solves it sequentially.

Preserving Plausibility. STRESSDREAM preserves plausibility by regularizing the optimized noise, encouraging it to remain in the typical set of the high-dimensional Gaussian. However, our notion of plausibility is defined with respect to the learned WM distribution: it assumes that generations from Gaussian noise are plausible under the WM. Thus, STRESSDREAM can keep steering within the outcomes the WM supports, but it cannot correct a base WM that already produces physically implausible generations. One could incorporate video-quality or physical-consistency metrics as part of the steering objective [86, 31, 42], to explicitly bias generations toward higher-quality imaginations. Conversely, STRESSDREAM also cannot imagine outcomes that may be plausible in the real world but are absent from the WM distribution. This highlights the importance of training WMs on diverse interaction data, including failures and rare outcomes, rather than success-only data [51], so that their predictive distributions can support policy evaluation and improvement.

Computational Inefficiency. Although STRESSDREAM is sample-efficient for generating plausible target outcomes compared to random sampling by leveraging dense VLM gradients, its runtime is still primarily bottlenecked by video WM generation, which can take several minutes per imagination with current models [11, 74, 12, 1]. Inference-time steering methods for text-to-image generation often reduce this cost with step-distilled or shortcut models that generate samples in one or a few denoising steps, making both noise optimization and gradient computation more tractable [31, 42]. Our method is orthogonal to these advances: as video WMs become faster, STRESSDREAM directly benefits from improved sampling efficiency, and shortcut models may enable more accurate gradient computations without relying on the current approximation. Another promising direction is to amortize the iterative optimization by learning a noise generator for fixed target outcomes, similar to Eyring et al. [42], Wagenmaker et al. [102], but enforcing the typical-set constraints in high-dimensional initial noise space remains challenging. Lastly, STRESSDREAM incurs additional overhead from backpropagating through the criterion function, making each optimization step more expensive (Appendix B.6 for more details). However, random sampling still requires evaluating generated samples with expensive verifiers, so systematic steering within a finite sampling budget remains useful for discovering high-impact outcomes from WMs.

Reward Hacking. Although our gradient-based optimization with the Vision-Language-Model’s gradient is empirically effective, it can sometimes introduce reward hacking, where the criterion increases without inducing meaningful changes in the generated video [31, 19, 54]. Prior work mitigates this issue by combining multiple reward functions or adding image-/video-quality metrics that directly penalize degenerate generations [31, 86]. STRESSDREAM can also incorporate these strategies. In practice, however, we observe that VLM-based criteria trained on internet-scale data are substantially more robust than reward functions learned from scratch: they tend to assign high scores only when the target event is semantically evident in the generated video, whereas taskspecific learned rewards are more susceptible to adversarial artifacts. This limitation may be further mitigated by more efficient video WM architectures with robust latent representations [73], shortcut/distilled diffusion models with fewer denoising steps [70], and stronger reward models with better video understanding and robustness to adversarial artifacts [56]. Moreover, STRESSDREAM requires specifying target high-impact outcomes with text prompts, which may need to be adapted for each scene, and the criterion’s effectiveness depends on prompt quality (see Appendix B.3).
