Abstract: Action-conditioned world models allow robots to predict the future consequences of candidate actions without additional physical interaction, supporting policy evaluation, planning, and data augmentation. We present Mask2Real-WM, a two-stage action-conditioned world model for dexterous manipulation that decouples pixel prediction into a dynamics model and a rendering model. The dynamics model predicts future segmentation masks from past masks and 23-DoF action sequences. The rendering model maps the predicted masks to photorealistic RGB using a ControlNet-augmented Stable Video Diffusion backbone. The smaller sim-to-real gap in segmentation space enables the dynamics model to benefit from large-scale pretraining on over 50 h of synthetic simulation data, followed by fine-tuning on fewer than 2.5 h of real demonstrations. Experiments on a dexterous pick-and-place benchmark show that mask conditioning and simulation pretraining are both required for per-DoF action controllability across all 23 degrees of freedom. In contrast, monolithic baselines capture broad hand and end-effector trajectories but do not reliably reflect fine-grained, per-joint action effects.

Keywords: World Models, Dexterous Manipulation, Simulation

## 1 Introduction

World models are increasingly used in robot learning $[1]$ . In this setting, they commonly take two forms: policy backbones (World Action Models: WAMs) that jointly predict actions and observations, and action-conditioned world models that predict future states given actions. The latter support policy evaluation $[2, 3]$ , planning $[4]$ , and dreaming $[5]$ .

Dexterous hands [6, 7, 8, 9, 10] enable more versatile manipulation than parallel-jaw grippers, but action-conditioned world models for dexterous manipulation remain challenging to build. The action space is high-dimensional (23 DoF in our setup), large-scale dexterous datasets are scarce [11, 12], and accurately predicting finger-object contacts remains difficult for existing video models, which often blur these regions.

We address these challenges with Mask2Real-WM, an action-conditioned world model trained on fewer than 2.5 h of real interaction. Mask2Real-WM decouples pixel prediction into two stages: (i) an action-conditioned dynamics model (WM1) that predicts future segmentation masks from past masks and the action sequence, and (ii) a rendering model (WM2) that produces photorealistic two-view RGB conditioned on the predicted masks (see Fig. 2). Because segmentation masks have a smaller sim-to-real gap than RGB images, WM1 can be pretrained on large amounts of synthetic simulation data and then fine-tuned with minimal real data. WM2 learns appearance from the small real dataset. This use of simulation pretraining is relatively unexplored for image-space world models. Experiments show that the resulting decomposition produces sharper predictions and improves fine-grained controllable video generation than monolithic baselines.

## Contributions.

1. We use segmentation space as an effective sim-to-real bridge, enabling large-scale synthetic pretraining of the dynamics model on over 50 h of simulation data.

2. We present Mask2Real-WM, a two-stage action-conditioned world model with a simulation-pretrained dynamics model (WM1) and a real-data rendering model (WM2).

3. Experiments show that simulation midtraining of the dynamics model improves action controllability, over a real-only baseline (0.68→0.85 ID, 0.51→0.73 OOD, see section 4.2). Fine-tuning on real data improves controllability further, to 0.95 ID and 0.87 OOD.

## 2 Related Work

Action-conditioned video generation. World models that predict future observations from actions have emerged across a range of domains. Genie $[13]$ learns interactive environments from unlabelled internet video, enabling exploration of game-like worlds through learned action representations inferred from video. NVIDIA's Cosmos $[14, 15]$ extends this approach to physical AI, training diffusion-transformer video models on diverse real-world footage for applications including autonomous driving and industrial inspection. WAN $[16]$ similarly provides open, large-scale video generation models that can serve as a foundation for downstream tasks. A common theme across these works is the use of diffusion or flow matching models, whose stable training dynamics and high sample quality have made them common backbones for image-space world models.

World models for robotic manipulation. Within robotic manipulation, world models can be divided into policy backbones (WAMs) and action-conditioned simulators $[1]$ . We focus on the latter: given an action sequence, the model predicts how the scene evolves without additional physical interaction. Ctrl-World $[2]$ initializes from Stable Video Diffusion $[17]$ and learns controllable manipulation rollouts for low-DoF parallel-jaw grippers. Extending this approach to dexterous hands increases the dimensionality of the action conditioning and requires richer training data. DexWM $[4]$ addresses dexterous manipulation directly by conditioning on frame-to-frame differences in 3D hand keypoints and camera poses. Its predictor is trained in DINOv2 feature space and requires a separately trained decoder to visualize rollouts, whereas Mask2Real-WM directly leverages a pretrained video-generation backbone to produce photorealistic two-view RGB rollouts.

Conditioning video generation on structural signals. ControlNet $[18]$ introduced auxiliary conditioning branches, such as depth, edges, and segmentation masks, to improve spatial fidelity in diffusion models. This approach was extended to video prediction by Wang et al. $[19]$ . Two concurrent works use a decomposition related to ours. Mask World Model (MWM) $[20]$ decouples mask prediction from image generation, but targets parallel-jaw grippers and is structured as a WAM rather than a controllable RGB simulator. BridgeV2W $[21]$ conditions on embodiment masks derived from URDF kinematics. It therefore bypasses learned mask dynamics and does not model robot-object interactions. In contrast, Mask2Real-WM operates in pixel space and uses segmentation masks as a structured intermediate representation for sim-to-real transfer. Experiments show that this design supports per-DoF action controllability across all 23 degrees of freedom of a dexterous hand.

## 3 Method

## 3.1 Problem Formulation

We consider the action-conditioned video prediction problem for dexterous manipulation from a multi-view setup. At time t, the observation is a tuple $o_{t} = (I_{t}, m_{t})$ , where $I_{t} \in R^{V \times 3 \times H \times W}$ is the RGB image across V camera views and $m_{t} \in R^{V \times 3 \times H \times W}$ is the corresponding segmentation mask as an RGB image with a 3-class color vocabulary (hand: green, object: red, background: black); synthetic masks are additionally blurred ( $\sigma = 1.5$ px) to soften rasterization edges and reduce the domain gap. The action $a_{t} \in R^{6+17}$ is the concatenation of the absolute 6-DoF end-effector pose and the absolute 17-DoF ORCA hand joint configuration [6].

Given a context window of k past observations and an action sequence covering both past and a future horizon of H steps, the goal is to model

$$
p \big (I _ {t + 1: t + H} \mid I _ {t - k: t}, m _ {t - k: t}, a _ {t - k: t + H} \big).\tag{1}
$$

We use V = 2, k = 5, and H = 5, and roll the model out autoregressively at inference to obtain arbitrarily long predictions. All images are processed at a resolution of $135 \times 240$ pixels, chosen to fit both diffusion passes within the memory budget of a single GPU during inference.

Factorization. Rather than predicting pixels directly, Mask2Real-WM decomposes the joint distribution into a mask-space dynamics term and an image-space rendering term:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p \big (I _ {t + 1: t + H}, m _ {t + 1: t + H} \mid I _ {t - k: t}, m _ {t - k: t}, a _ {t - k: t + H} \big) \\ = \underbrace {p \big (m _ {t + 1 : t + H} \mid m _ {t - k : t} , a _ {t - k : t + H} \big)} _ {\text { WM1: dynamics }} \cdot \underbrace {p \big (I _ {t + 1 : t + H} \mid I _ {t - k : t} , m _ {t - k : t + H} , a _ {t - k : t + H} \big)} _ {\text { WM2: rendering }}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

This factorization is attractive because (i) the sim-to-real gap is substantially smaller in mask space than in RGB, enabling large-scale synthetic pretraining of WM1, and (ii) off-the-shelf segmentors such as SAM 3 [22] provide accurate masks from text prompts alone, supervising the intermediate representation at scale without label noise.

## 3.2 Data Pipeline

We use IsaacLab $[24]$ to generate over 50 h of synthetic demonstrations with ground-truth segmentation labels via a MimicGen-based pipeline $[23]$ and an exploratory sinusoidal joint generator (see Appendix J); PD gains and friction parameters are tuned to match the physical robot $[25]$ (calibration in Appendix K). For real data, we collect fewer than 2.5 h of ORCA-hand $[6]$ demonstrations using Rokoko glove $[26]$ for teleoperation and two synchronized camera views; masks are generated automatically by Segment Anything (SAM) 3 $[22]$ using text prompts, with a short auxiliary clip bootstrapping the tracker for the occlusion-heavy wrist view.

## 3.3 Stage 1: Dynamics Model (WM1)

WM1 (left side of Figure 2) is a video diffusion model initialized from SVD [17], adopting the multi-view, action-conditioned formulation of Ctrl-World [2]. It predicts future segmentation masks from past masks and actions, freeing it from modelling lighting, texture, and background appearance that differ between simulation and reality.

Architecture. At each denoising step, the past-segmentation VAE latents at frames $t-k,\ldots,t$ are concatenated with the noisy future latents at $t+1,\ldots,t+H$ along the time dimension, forming a tensor of shape $(B,k+H,C,V\cdot H/f,W/f)$ , where f=8 is the spatial compression factor of the VAE and the two views are stacked along the height dimension. The independently normalized 6-DoF end-effector pose and 17 hand joint angles are projected by separate MLPs into 1024-dimensional per-frame embeddings, summed, and injected into every SVD U-Net block via frame-wise cross-attention. WM1 is pretrained on simulation data then fine-tuned on real data with LoRA [27] (rank 16, $\alpha = 16$ ); training details are in Appendix L. The denoising objective is

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{WM1}} = \mathbb {E} _ {x, \epsilon , \sigma} \big \| \hat {x} _ {\theta} (x _ {\sigma}; m _ {t - k: t}, a _ {t - k: t + H}, \sigma) - x \big \| _ {2} ^ {2}.\tag{3}
$$

## 3.4 Stage 2: Appearance Model (WM2)

WM2 shares the SVD backbone of WM1 but operates in RGB space and is conditioned on the future masks predicted by WM1. It is trained exclusively on real data (fewer than 2.5 h), since appearance is where the sim-to-real gap is, and the dynamics is learned by WM1.

Architecture. WM2 (right side of Figure 2) receives three conditioning signals: past RGB frames encoded through SVD's VAE; segmentation masks (past and future) processed by a lightweight CNN encoder followed by a ControlNet module [18]; and actions via the same dual-branch MLP cross-attention as WM1. The CNN encoder operates in pixel space on decoded mask images, specializing in hand and object boundaries that the VAE encoder would blur (see Appendix H). ControlNet further processes these features and injects them into SVD's encoder and middle blocks through zero-initialized convolutions at each resolution level. The SVD backbone is frozen and adapted via LoRA [27] (rank 16, $\alpha = 16$ ); the CNN encoder and ControlNet are fully fine-tuned. Independent mask and action conditional dropout [28] (each at $10\%$ ) enables classifier-free guidance at inference. Training details are in Appendix L. The denoising objective is

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{WM2}} = \mathbb {E} _ {x, \epsilon , \sigma} \big \| \hat {x} _ {\phi} (x _ {\sigma}; I _ {t - k: t}, m _ {t - k: t + H}, a _ {t - k: t + H}, \sigma) - x \big \| _ {2} ^ {2}.\tag{4}
$$

## 3.5 Inference

At inference, SAM 3 first processes a 5-frame context window of real RGB observations to obtain the initial segmentation masks, enabling WM1 to rollout future mask sequences for the ORCA arena (Figure 20) environment; WM2 then renders photorealistic RGB conditioned on the predicted masks, past frames, and actions. For long-horizon prediction, the final predicted frame and mask slide into the context window, replacing SAM 3 for subsequent autoregressive chunks.

## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

Our real-world platform combines a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda arm with the 17-DoF Orca hand [6] as the end-effector, for a total of 23 DoF. The scene is observed from two RGB views: a fixed workspace camera and a wrist-mounted camera rigidly attached to the Orca palm that provides a close-up view of finger-object contacts.

Arena. The workspace is a custom arena with tilted, colored walls in which the robot performs a continuous pick-and-place task with a set of small everyday objects. This setup is deliberately designed to support a broad range of experiments with a single physical configuration: (i) the inclined walls cause objects to roll back toward the center, enabling continuous, uninterrupted data collection as well as unscripted play data where the hand freely interacts with objects that never leave the workspace; (ii) the colored walls and floor provide rich, spatially-varying visual texture, which yields more discriminative perceptual metrics than uniform backgrounds; (iii) the arena can be rotated to expose the policy and the world model to different backgrounds and lighting incidences; and (iv) the object set can be swapped to probe generalization across shapes, sizes, and materials.

Simulation counterpart. Because WM1 operates on segmentation masks, simulation only needs to be geometrically and kinematically faithful; photorealism is irrelevant. We therefore build a minimal Isaac Sim counterpart, reusing the default table asset together with the Franka and Orca USD models, and forgo the expensive material, lighting, and texture tuning that image-space sim-to-real pipelines typically require. Ground-truth masks are read directly from the simulator's instance segmentation output. Further details on both the real-world rig and the simulation assets are provided in Appendix K.

Training details. WM1 is pretrained for 55,000 steps ( $lr\ 10^{-4}$ ) on a single H200 GPU, then fine-tuned for 45,000 steps ( $lr\ 5\times10^{-6}$ ) on a single H200; WM2 is trained for 70,000 steps ( $lr\ 10^{-4}$ ) on 8 H100 GPUs. Full hyperparameter tables are in Appendix L.

## 4.2 Action Controllability

Protocol. We apply a sinusoidal signal to one action component at a time while holding all others fixed, scoring each perturbed sequence on a three-point scale (1: expected motion with no spurious coupling; 0.5: motion present but with coupled articulations; 0: no response) on 5 in-distribution (ID) and 5 out-of-distribution (OOD) samples by an independent evaluator, and report the mean score across all action dimensions.

Results. Figure 4 summarizes the results. Our full model (WM1: sim→real, WM2: real) achieves a controllability score of ≈0.95 on ID frames and ≈0.87 on OOD frames, substantially outperforming all ablated variants and the monolithic baseline [2] (≈0.6 ID, ≈0.44 OOD). Crucially, isolated individual-finger motion is absent from the real-world training set, which predominantly contains cube-grasping demonstrations; without simulation pretraining, the model therefore lacks any exposure to independent per-finger actuation. Training WM1 on simulation data alone raises controllability markedly (from ≈0.68 ID and ≈0.51 OOD for the real-only baseline to ≈0.85 ID and ≈0.73 OOD), because the large-scale synthetic data covers the full joint range of every finger and exposes the model to motions absent from the limited real dataset. Fine-tuning WM1 on real data aligns its mask predictions with the SAM3 mask distribution used to train WM2, reducing the train–test mismatch at inference. Qualitative inspection of the generated videos confirms that the full model actuates each finger joint across a wider angular range than the baseline, with no visible coupling between independently controlled joints.

## 4.3 Video Prediction Quality

Figure 5 reports PSNR ↑ [29], SSIM ↑ [30], and LPIPS ↓ [31] for three WM1 training configurations (real-only, simulation-pretrained, and sim-then-real, ours), all evaluated with the same WM2 on ID and three OOD test sets: No Object (arena without a manipulation target), Random Play (unstructured interaction), and Background (rotated arena, unseen lighting incidence). Long-horizon rollout frame sequences are provided in Appendix M.

The real-only WM1 variant yields the highest ID pixel metrics because it adapts fully to the appearance distribution of the real training set. However, it degrades most severely under OOD conditions, reflecting that without large-scale synthetic exposure the dynamics model overfits to the specific visual and kinematic statistics of the training environment. Pretraining WM1 on simulation data substantially reduces OOD degradation by exposing the model to a broader range of joint configurations and object poses, and subsequent real fine-tuning (our full model) further closes the gap while maintaining strong ID performance. Qualitative long-horizon rollouts are provided in Appendix M.

An extended video-quality analysis on all seven splits—per-frame sharpness, action controllability, and the effect of simulation midtraining—is provided in Appendix A.

Figure 6 ablates the two WM2 conditioning signals. Mask conditioning via ControlNet is the main driver of spatial quality: removing it yields blurry, temporally averaged outputs even when LPIPS remains competitive, whereas masks alone recover sharp hand and object boundaries. This mask-only formulation also allows WM2 to be deployed without access to the robot action space. Adding action conditioning further improves perceptual quality, particularly in the wrist view, where scale remains ambiguous from segmentation masks alone.

## 5 Discussion

The Ctrl-World baseline achieves higher ID pixel metrics because a monolithic model memorizes the appearance of its training distribution rather than learning to respond faithfully to actions, which explains its poor controllability ( $\approx0.6$ ID, $\approx0.44$ OOD) and severe OOD degradation ( $6\times$ LPIPS increase vs. $2.8\times$ for ours). Our mask-conditioned model trades some ID pixel accuracy for structural generalization: WM1 predicts layouts in a nearly domain-invariant space, so mask fidelity holds even under background changes or absent objects, while WM2 only renders appearance onto an already-correct structure. Sim pretraining is essential because synthetic data covers the full angular range of every finger joint, enabling decoupled action-faithful responses; real fine-tuning then closes the gap for near-grasp configurations. A separate WM2 conditioning ablation in Appendix C shows that mask conditioning is the primary driver of spatial sharpness, while action conditioning further tightens motion consistency.

The two-stage design also provides practical modularity: WM1 can serve as a lightweight dynamics checker without running WM2, and WM2 can be fine-tuned for a new scene by updating only its ControlNet weights. A current constraint is the fixed-camera assumption; conditioning on intrinsics and extrinsics would remove it.

Limitations. All principal failure modes manifest in WM1. Without depth information, WM1 loses objects under hand occlusion; depth conditioning is a natural remedy. Over long horizons, the color-class mask provides no object-level binding, causing identity drift addressable via text-prompt or object-ID conditioning. The synthetic data does not fully cover natural manipulation motions; richer generators (RL, trajectory optimization, or video retargeting) would improve coverage. Finally, two sequential diffusion passes are expensive; distillation into consistency models $[32]$ is a promising route to near-real-time inference. Qualitative failure examples are in Appendix E.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented Mask2Real-WM, a two-stage action-conditioned world model for dexterous manipulation using segmentation masks as a sim-to-real bridge. Pretraining the dynamics model (WM1) on over 50 h of synthetic data and fine-tuning on fewer than 2.5 h of real demonstrations yields strong per-DoF controllability without large-scale real data; the ControlNet-augmented appearance model (WM2) then renders photorealistic video from predicted masks. Mask2Real-WM outperforms monolithic baselines in controllability and out-of-distribution robustness, and we believe the segmentation-space bridge is broadly applicable wherever large-scale real data is scarce.

## E Failure Mode Examples

We provide qualitative examples of the failure modes described in Section 5. They share a common root cause: the current bottleneck is WM1's object-state prediction. WM1 reliably captures hand dynamics but is less certain about the object's state, producing two characteristic errors. The first is object vanishing: when the hand occludes the object for several steps, WM1 drops it from the predicted masks, after which WM2 renders an empty scene (Figure 13); the object often reappears once the occlusion clears. The second is object duplication / spawning: WM1 hallucinates a second instance of the object or re-spawns it at a wrong location (Figure 14). Both originate in WM1's dynamics rather than WM2's rendering: when ground-truth masks are substituted, WM2 renders the object correctly (Appendix G).

## H CNN Mask Encoder vs. VAE Encoder for ControlNet Conditioning

To justify using a dedicated CNN encoder rather than the SVD VAE encoder to condition ControlNet, we analyse the features each encoder produces from identical segmentation maps, following the convolutional-feature visualisation methodology of Zeiler and Fergus $[36]$ . We compute a boundary-response score—the ratio of feature-gradient magnitude at segmentation class boundaries to that in flat interior regions (a value > 1 indicates boundary-selective features, in the spirit of learned edge detectors $[37]$ ). Across 128 evaluation frames, the CNN adapter attains a mean boundary response of $4.41 \pm 0.74$ versus $2.98 \pm 0.43$ for the VAE encoder (Figure 19), i.e. the CNN encodes class boundaries roughly 48% more strongly, on every frame. The PCA visualisation confirms this: CNN features exhibit crisp, well-localised boundaries with clean interiors, whereas the VAE features are noisier and smear across segmentation edges.

## J Simulation Data Collection

We generate synthetic dexterous-manipulation data in IsaacLab $[24]$ using two complementary data generators: a MimicGen-based demonstration generator $[23]$ and a procedural exploration generator. Both generators provide ground-truth segmentation labels directly from simulation.

MimicGen-based generation. We adapt the MimicGen pipeline $[23]$ to our dexterous-hand setup. We first collect approximately 100 source demonstrations in simulation through teleoperation with an Apple Vision Pro. MimicGen uses these source trajectories to generate additional demonstrations in new scene configurations. Specifically, it decomposes each source demonstration into object-centric subtask segments, transforms the end-effector trajectory of each segment relative to the corresponding object pose in the new scene, and executes the resulting segments sequentially.

To apply MimicGen, we specify the boundaries of each subtask using simple reward signals, such as whether the hand is sufficiently close to the target object. We apply small sinusoidal perturbations to the finger joints while replaying pre-grasp segments to increase variation in the hand configurations. We do not perturb the fingers during the grasping segment, which is replayed consistently to preserve successful grasps. This reliance on manually specified, reward-detectable subtask boundaries is appropriate for our pick-and-place setup, but may become limiting for more complex tasks whose stages cannot be identified by simple geometric or contact-based signals.

## K Implementation Details

Real-world rig. The physical setup (Figure 20) comprises a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda arm equipped with the 17-DoF ORCA hand (23 actuated DoF in total), operating inside a fixed arena. Two Luxonis OAK-D RGB cameras provide the observations: a static third-person camera overlooking the workspace and a wrist-mounted camera rigidly attached to the hand.

![](images/080a114538b509b96cfd81616860f9cfa58d4092344d18b7ee58bb8ea636de48.jpg)  
Figure 20: Real-world rig. Franka Panda arm with the ORCA hand in the arena, observed by a third-person and a wrist-mounted RGB camera.

Simulation assets. The simulation uses the default Franka Emika Panda URDF and the ORCA hand USD. The arena geometry is reconstructed from physical measurements and exported as a USD asset. No material, lighting, or texture tuning is applied.

System identification. To reduce the kinematic gap between the simulated ORCA hand and the physical robot, we perform system identification following Bjelonic et al. $[25]$ : for each joint, PD gains and friction coefficients are optimized with CMA-ES to minimize the tracking error between the simulated response and the real trajectory under a chirp (increasing-frequency sinusoid) command. After calibration the simulated trajectory closely follows the measured one across the full frequency sweep, which is sufficient for the joint-angle-driven segmentation representation to remain accurate.

SAM 3 prompt engineering. For the third-person view, the prompts "hand" and the object name (e.g., "red cube") are used. For the wrist view, a short initialization clip is prepended in which the hand moves clearly into frame (Figure 21); the bounding box extracted from this clip seeds SAM 3's tracker for the remainder of the sequence.

![](images/9807b61e8ad422ab8e40bd21eee19ed0729559ac7fcc955a8419267781227375.jpg)  
Figure 21: Wrist-view SAM 3 initialization clip. Example frames (left to right) in which the hand moves into the wrist camera's field of view; the hand bounding box from this clip seeds the tracker for the rest of the sequence.

CNN mask encoder for ControlNet. The CNN encoder consists of three convolutional blocks (Conv2d + GroupNorm + SiLU) with a strided downsampling layer followed by a transposed convolution to restore spatial resolution, and a final $1 \times 1$ convolution to match the ControlNet feature dimension. Operating on decoded pixel-space masks rather than VAE latents is key: the VAE encoder was not designed for binary/categorical segmentation maps and introduces reconstruction artifacts at class boundaries, whereas a CNN can specialize in detecting the clean edges present in segmentation images—convolutional features are well established as boundary- and edge-selective representations [36, 37]. A detailed ablation supporting this choice is in Appendix H.

## M Future Work and Policy Rollout Videos

Beyond the directions in Section 5, several extensions are worth pursuing. Scaling the simulation data generator with reinforcement learning or trajectory-optimization policies would cover a broader range of contact-rich manipulations. Conditioning the model on camera intrinsics and extrinsics would remove the fixed-viewpoint constraint and enable deployment across diverse setups without re-collecting data. Extending the segmentation color vocabulary to multiple objects would unlock multi-object manipulation. Another key next step is increasing inference speed: the two sequential diffusion passes are expensive, so distillation techniques such as consistency models $[32]$ that distill WM1 and WM2 into few-step samplers are a promising route to near-real-time rollouts.

Policy rollouts in imagination. We roll out three policies trained on the real robot—a flow-matching policy, a diffusion policy, and an ACT policy, all trained on red-cube grasps and placements onto the red ramp—inside both Mask2Real-WM and the baseline world model (Figures 22–24). Rollouts are generated autoregressively at 5 fps with a context window of k = 5 frames and a prediction horizon of H = 5 frames per chunk. Using Mask2Real-WM as a policy evaluator—scoring candidate policies by their predicted outcomes in imagination—is a natural extension we leave to future work.
