# SCAR: Self-Supervised Continuous Action Representation Learning

Hongjia Liu<sup>1,2</sup>, Fan Feng<sup>1</sup>, Minghao Fu<sup>1</sup>, Xinyue Wang<sup>1</sup>, Haofei Lu<sup>2</sup>, Biwei Huang<sup>1</sup> <sup>1</sup>University of California, San Diego <sup>2</sup>KTH Royal Institute of Technology

## Abstract

Despite the central role of action in embodied intelligence, learning transferable action representations from visual transitions remains a fundamental challenge, particularly when world models must generalize across embodiments under limited data. We argue that action is not merely an auxiliary conditioning signal, but a distinct representational factor that decouples the controllable change from embodimentspecific actuation. In this work, we propose SCAR, a joint inverse–forward dynamics framework for learning unified action representations across embodiments from visual transitions. Built on a pretrained generative backbone, SCAR uses an inverse dynamics model (IDM) to infer latent actions from latent observation pairs and a forward dynamics model (FDM) to predict future dynamics conditioned on them. To make the latent space transferable rather than a generic visual bottleneck, we regularize the latent action posterior toward a standard Gaussian prior to limit arbitrary visual encoding, and introduce adversarial invariance to suppress embodiment- and environment-specific nuisance factors. Experiments on the Procgen and Robotwin dataset show that the learned unified latent action representation serves as a stronger conditioning interface for world modeling than embodimentspecific raw actions, yielding improved cross-embodiment low-data adaptation and cross-task transfer. Taken together, these results suggest that action can be learned as a shared representation of controllable change across embodiments, providing an interface for more transferable and generalizable world models.

## 1 Introduction

Embodied agents must represent not only what is present in the scene but also how the body can act to change it. In cognitive science and neuroscience, this action-oriented aspect is discussed through the notion of body schema: a sensorimotor representation that supports action planning and control, distinct from purely perceptual or semantic descriptions of the body [1–4]. Such representations are not rigidly tied to a single morphology; they can adapt under tool use [5, 6], and studies of imitation suggest that action content can be partially dissociated from the specific limb used to realize it [7–9].

In embodied AI, however, the action signal used to train world models is usually tied to a specific body and command interface. Recorded actions are imperfect proxies for the physical interventions that drive state change: control dynamics, contact, delays, and calibration differences mediate command execution, so the same nominal action can yield different transitions across embodiments or controllers [10, 11]. This issue is especially problematic for cross-embodiment world modeling, where the shared intervention structure is easily obscured by substantial embodiment-specific realization details [12]. This raises a central question: can we condition world models on an action representation learned from visual transitions, one that captures how an agent intervenes in the world without being tied to a specific body or raw command space?

Recent latent-action models [13–17] learn action-like representations from visual transitions following an inverse-forward dynamics framework: an inverse dynamics model (IDM) infers latent action from visual transitions, while a forward dynamics model (FDM) is trained to reconstruct future observations conditioned on these latent actions. Many methods aim to unlock unlabeled video data for policy learning by learning compact action codes with little or no action supervision [13, 14]. In contrast, our goal is to learn a unified action representation that can serve as a transferable conditioning interface for world models across embodied domains. However, learning such a representation from video is challenging because the latent may encode appearance, task identity, or embodiment-specific statistics instead of controllable change [18]. It is further exacerbated when the FDM must learn visual dynamics from scratch, as reconstruction losses can dominate action abstraction; see Appendix D.

We therefore propose SCAR, a self-supervised inverse–forward dynamics framework for learning unified continuous action representations across embodiments. The key idea is to treat action as the part of a visual transition that explains controllable change, rather than as the embodiment-specific command used to produce it. A useful latent action should therefore retain the information needed for future prediction, while avoiding two shortcuts: it should not simply compress future visual details, and it should not encode embodiment-specific appearance or actuation statistics. The representation should also connect back to control, since a transition-side latent inferred from future observations is only useful at deployment if it can be recovered from raw commands and the current visual context.

SCAR implements these desiderata in the latent space of a pretrained causal VAE. The IDM infers a stochastic latent action z<sub>t</sub> from a realized visual transition, while the FDM, initialized from the Wan video generative backbone [19], predicts future latent dynamics conditioned on $z _ { t } ,$ forming a latent-action-conditioned world model. To prevent above two shortcuts, we introduce KL regularization to limit the information capacity of the latent action posterior, and adversarial invariance via gradient reversal [20] to remove embodiment-identifying factors. Because ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ is inferred from future observations during representation learning, we further train a lightweight context-conditioned action-to-latent controller that maps raw action sequences and visual context back into the learned latent action space. This separates representation discovery from embodiment-specific command realization: SCAR first learns what controllable change is shared across embodiments, and then learns how each embodiment realizes it through its own commands.

We evaluate the resulting latent action interface on Procgen [21] and Robotwin [22]. Procgen tests environment transfer with a shared discrete action space, while Robotwin evaluates crossembodiment transfer in continuous-control manipulation. Experiments show that the learned latent representation provides a stronger world-model conditioning interface than raw actions, improving low-data adaptation and transfer-task generalization. Fig. 1 further shows that latent actions inferred from one embodiment can drive another embodiment’s visual context, suggesting that SCAR learns transferable action structure across bodies. We also show that controllability is recovered more effectively through context-conditioned sequence-level action-to-latent prediction than through direct raw-action conditioning. Together, these results support a simple claim: the transferable part of action is not the embodiment-specific command, but the intervention structure that explains how the world changes, and this representation enables more generalizable world models.

Contributions. Our contributions are threefold. (1) We formulate cross-embodiment world-model conditioning as a transition-side action representation problem, replacing embodiment-specific raw commands with a learned interface grounded in realized visual change. (2) We propose SCAR, an inverse–forward latent dynamics framework that learns compact and embodiment-invariant continuous latent actions from visual transitions. (3) We show that the learned latent interface improves low-data prediction and transfer, reduces embodiment leakage, and can be connected back to raw commands through an action-to-latent controller.

## 2 Related Work

Action-conditioned world models. Robotic world models typically condition future prediction on recorded actions, either in image space [23], latent visual space [24], or learned dynamics representations [25, 26]. Such action-conditioned models provide an interface for planning [25, 27] and policy learning [28–30], but recorded actions are tied to a specific embodiment, controller, and action parameterization [12]. SCAR instead treats the conditioning interface itself as a representation learning problem, using transition-side latent actions to condition a generative world model.

Latent action learning from visual transitions. Recent latent-action methods infer action-like codes from visual transitions with an inverse dynamics model and train a forward model or video predictor to ensure that these codes explain future observations [13–15]. Many methods learn discrete action tokens, which can be limiting for fine-grained continuous manipulation. Continuous latentaction methods such as CLAM [16], VILA [31], and VILLA-X [32] introduce richer action spaces, but often rely on action labels, proprioception, or other supervision to anchor the latent. Other works scale latent-action learning with in-the-wild videos or VLA pretraining [17, 33]. However, transition prediction alone can yield latents that encode appearance, task, or embodiment shortcuts rather than controllable change [34, 18]. SCAR addresses this issue through capacity regularization and embodiment-adversarial invariance, aiming to make the latent action more suitable for crossembodiment world-model conditioning.

Cross-embodiment robot learning. Large-scale robot learning must handle heterogeneous morphologies, sensors, and control spaces [12]. Existing approaches reduce this heterogeneity through multi-embodiment pretraining [35–37], manually defined shared command abstractions such as human hand skeletons [38–40], or adversarial alignment of visual representations [41, 42]. These methods primarily align policies, visual features, or predefined state–action interfaces. In contrast, SCAR learns the action-conditioning interface itself as a transition-side continuous latent representation, directly from realized visual dynamics across embodiments.

## 3 Method

## 3.1 Problem Formulation: Learning a Shared Latent Action Interface

We consider trajectories collected from multiple embodiments, $\boldsymbol { \mathcal { D } } = \{ ( \boldsymbol { x } _ { 1 : T } , \boldsymbol { a } _ { 1 : T - 1 } ^ { e } , e ) \}$ , where $\pmb { x } _ { 1 : T }$ is a video sequence, $\pmb { a } _ { 1 : T - 1 } ^ { e }$ is the embodiment-specific command sequence, and $e \in \mathcal { E }$ denotes the embodiment identity. In cross-embodiment world modeling, raw commands are often a poor common interface: the same command can induce different physical effects under different morphologies, controllers, delays, and contact dynamics, while different actions may produce similar visual transitions. We therefore seek an inferred unified latent action ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ that is learned from realized visual transitions and captures the controllable change shared across embodiments.

From a data-generation perspective, the key distinction is between the shared effect of an action and the embodiment-specific way in which this effect is realized. We write $\mathbf { \pmb { u } } _ { t }$ for the unobserved ground-truth unified latent action (the embodiment-invariant controllable factor in the data-generating process); z<sub>t</sub> is the model’s estimation of $\mathbf { \Delta } \mathbf { u } _ { t }$ , recovered from visual transitions and used to condition the world model. A raw command can be viewed as an embodiment-specific realization $\pmb { a } _ { t } ^ { e } = h ( \pmb { u } _ { t } , e )$ , which drives the transition $\pmb { s } _ { t + 1 } = \mathscr { F } ( \pmb { s } _ { t } , \pmb { a } _ { t } ^ { e } )$ and gives the observation $\pmb { x } _ { t } = R ( \pmb { s } _ { t } )$ . An inverse dynamics model can therefore recover from $\mathbf { \Phi } ( \pmb { x } _ { t } , \pmb { x } _ { t + 1 } )$ an action coordinate $\tilde { \pmb { a } } _ { t } = I _ { \phi } ( \pmb { x } _ { t } , \pmb { x } _ { t + 1 } )$ that explains the realized change. However, since $\tilde { \mathbf { a } } _ { t }$ is recovered through the embodiment-specific transition, it may still contain embodiment-dependent bias. The goal of SCAR is to keep the transition-predictive part of this coordinate while removing the part that identifies the embodiment with a discriminative objective, yielding the inferred unified latent action ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ for world-model conditioning.

In principle, SCAR gives the sufficient conditions for recovering unified latent actions: under a structured data-generation process, prediction can recover an action coordinate from visual change, and adversarial embodiment removal can select the shared controllable component. The full formal statement and proof are provided in Appendix $\mathrm { A } .$

Theorem 1 (Informal: sufficient conditions for unified latent action recovery). Suppose each trajectory is generated from embodiment-invariant ground-truth unified latent actions $\mathbf { \pmb { u } } _ { t }$ through an embodiment-specific realization ${ \pmb a } _ { t } ^ { e } = h ( { \pmb u } _ { t } , e )$ , followed by $\pmb { s } _ { t + 1 } = \mathcal { F } ( \pmb { s } _ { t } , \pmb { a } _ { t } ^ { e } )$ and $\pmb { x } _ { t } = R ( \pmb { s } _ { t } )$ Assume these maps are injective on the data support. If the IDM recovers the raw action $\pmb { a } _ { t } ^ { e }$ up to an invertible reparameterization, $\tilde { \mathbf { } { \boldsymbol { a } } } _ { t } = \rho ( \mathbf { \boldsymbol { a } } _ { t } ^ { e } )$ , and this recovered action coordinate can be decomposed into a shared controllable component and an embodiment-specific component, then an encoder trained with a population-level discriminative objective removes the embodiment-specific component and recovers the unified latent action space up to a per-embodiment invertible bijection ${ \boldsymbol z } _ { t } = \Phi _ { e } ( { \boldsymbol u } _ { t } )$ Therefore, z provides a unified latent action interface for cross-embodiment world models.

Theorem 1 provides a data-generation view of a unified latent action. We instantiate this principle end-to-end in the latent space of a pretrained video model: the IDM infers a stochastic latent action sequence $z _ { 1 : T - 1 }$ from latent video transitions, and the FDM predicts future latent dynamics conditioned on it. However, prediction alone is insufficient, since a predictive latent may still encode future visual details or embodiment-specific nuisance information. Thus, Sec. 3.2 introduces the inverse–forward learning objective, while Sec. 3.3 adds KL and GRL regularization: KL limits the capacity of ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ to prevent unrestricted visual coding, and GRL discourages embodiment-discriminative information. Together, these components preserve transition-predictive controllable information while suppressing embodiment-specific shortcuts.

## 3.2 Learning Predictive Latent Actions with an Inverse–Forward Model

As illustrated in Fig. 2, SCAR operates in the latent space of a pretrained Wan video generation model [19]. Given an input video window $\pmb { x } _ { 1 : T }$ , we first encode it with a frozen causal VAE, ${ \pmb v } _ { 1 : F } = E _ { \mathrm { v a e } } ( { \pmb x } _ { 1 : T } )$ , where ${ \boldsymbol { v } } _ { 1 : F }$ denotes the temporally compressed latent representation. Operating in this latent space provides a structured representation of visual dynamics while avoiding the redundancy of raw pixels.

The inverse dynamics model infers a transition-level latent action sequence from the latent video:

$$
\boldsymbol {z} _ {1: T - 1} = I _ {\phi} (\boldsymbol {v} _ {1: F}).\tag{1}
$$

We parameterize the IDM output as a diagonal Gaussian posterior, $q _ { \phi } \big ( z _ { 1 : T - 1 } \big | \big | \begin{array} { c } { { \boldsymbol { v } _ { 1 : F } } } \end{array} \big )$ = $\begin{array} { r l } {  { \prod _ { t = 1 } ^ { T - 1 } \mathcal { N } \big ( z _ { t } ; \pmb { \mu } _ { t } , \mathrm { d i a g } ( \pmb { \sigma } _ { t } ^ { 2 } ) \big ) } \qquad } & { { } } \end{array}$ , and sample $z _ { t } = \mu _ { t } + \sigma _ { t } \odot \epsilon _ { t }$ , with $\epsilon _ { t } \sim \mathcal { N } ( 0 , I )$ , during training. At inference time, we use the posterior mean $\pmb { \mu } _ { t }$

The FDM predicts future latent dynamics conditioned on the inferred latent action. We map the frame-rate latent action sequence $z _ { 1 : T - 1 }$ to a conditioning sequence aligned with the latent-video,

$$
\boldsymbol {c} _ {1: F} = A _ {\psi} (\boldsymbol {z} _ {1: T - 1}),\tag{2}
$$

and inject $c _ { f }$ into the pretrained Wan backbone through adaptive layer normalization:

$$
\left(\boldsymbol {\beta} _ {f}, \boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {f}\right) = W _ {\mathrm{mod}} \boldsymbol {c} _ {f}, \quad \operatorname{AdaLN} (\boldsymbol {h}; \boldsymbol {c} _ {f}) = (1 + \boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {f}) \odot \operatorname{LN} (\boldsymbol {h}) + \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {f}.\tag{3}
$$

This inverse–forward coupling forces the latent action to carry information that is useful for predicting the realized transition.

## 3.3 Preventing Shortcut Latents: Compactness and Embodiment Invariance

As suggested by Theorem 1, prediction alone does not guarantee that the latent action captures controllable change. In cross-embodiment data, the IDM may reduce reconstruction loss by encoding shortcuts such as embodiment identity, robot appearance, camera configuration, or task-specific visual context. SCAR therefore constrains the latent action with two complementary regularizers: a GRL objective that removes embodiment-discriminative information, and a KL objective that limits the capacity of the latent code.

![](images/d1b0786498c8a1183356150f958699a0f8891d65531f17777571217bf87c942c.jpg)  
Figure 2: Overview of SCAR. During training, a frozen Wan encoder maps video frames into latent space; the IDM infers stochastic latent actions, and the FDM predicts future latent dynamics conditioned on them. KL constrains latent capacity and GRL suppresses embodiment-specific information. At inference time, an action-to-latent controller maps raw commands and visual context to latent actions for controllable prediction.

Embodiment invariance via gradient reversal. To suppress embodiment-specific shortcuts, we attach an adversarial embodiment classifier to the latent action. Each latent token is passed through a gradient reversal layer before classification, and the adversarial loss is defined as:

$$
\hat {e} _ {i, t} = D _ {\omega} (R _ {\alpha} (\pmb {z} _ {i, t})), \qquad \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRL}} = \frac {1}{| \Omega |} \sum_ {(i, t) \in \Omega} \mathrm{CE} (\hat {e} _ {i, t}, e _ {i}).\tag{4}
$$

The classifier is trained to predict the embodiment identity, while the IDM receives the reversed gradient and is encouraged to remove embodiment-discriminative information from ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t } .$ . Appendix A provides a sufficient-condition analysis showing that, under idealized structured-nuisance assumptions and a non-collapsed predictive encoder, the adversarial objective removes embodiment-dependent directions while preserving a transition-predictive latent subspace.

Compactness via KL regularization. To prevent ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ from becoming an unconstrained visual compression code, we regularize the posterior toward a standard Gaussian prior:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{KL}} = \frac {1}{T - 1} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T - 1} D _ {\mathrm{KL}} \left(q _ {\phi} \left(\boldsymbol {z} _ {t} \mid \boldsymbol {v} _ {1: F}\right) \| \mathcal {N} (0, I)\right).\tag{5}
$$

This limits the capacity of the latent action and makes it harder to hide nuisance visual or embodiment specific details in a high-dimensional code.

Why the two regularizers are complementary. For a fixed latent-action encoder, the optimal embodiment classifier in the GRL branch satisfies

$$
\min _ {\omega} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRL}} (\omega , \phi) = H (e \mid \boldsymbol {z}) = H (e) - I (e; \boldsymbol {z}).\tag{6}
$$

Thus, reversing the gradient of $L _ { \mathrm { G R I } }$ encourages the encoder to reduce the mutual information between the latent action ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ and the embodiment identity e. However, adversarial invariance alone is not sufficient: a collapsed latent would also make $I ( e ; z )$ small. The prediction loss prevents this trivial solution by forcing ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ to explain the realized transition, while $L _ { \mathrm { K I } }$ limits latent capacity and L<sub>GRL</sub> removes embodiment-discriminative information.

In summary, the three pressures play distinct roles: prediction (Sec. 3.2) keeps $z _ { t }$ transition-relevant, KL makes ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ compact, and GRL makes ${ \boldsymbol { z } } _ { t }$ embodiment-invariant. Together, they encourage a latent action that captures controllable change rather than visual or embodiment-specific shortcuts.

## 3.4 Recovering Controllability from Raw Commands

The transition-side latent action is inferred from visual changes during representation learning. At inference time, however, a controllable world model must be driven by raw command sequences. We therefore learn a context-conditioned action-to-latent controller that maps raw actions and visual context to the learned latent action space:

$$
\pmb {h} _ {1: F _ {\mathrm{ctx}}} = E _ {\xi} (\pmb {v} _ {1: F _ {\mathrm{ctx}}}), \qquad \hat {\pmb {z}} _ {1: T - 1} = D _ {\eta} (\pmb {a} _ {1: T - 1}, \pmb {h} _ {1: F _ {\mathrm{ctx}}}),\tag{7}
$$

where $\pmb { v } _ { 1 : F _ { \mathrm { c t x } } }$ denotes observed context latents, $E _ { \xi }$ encodes them as key-value memory, and $D _ { \eta }$ is a transformer decoder that internally projects raw actions as query inputs to predict latent actions.

The controller is trained to match the frozen IDM latent actions:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{A2L}} = \frac {1}{| \Omega |} \sum_ {t \in \Omega} \| \hat {\boldsymbol {z}} _ {t} - \mathrm{sg} (\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {\phi , t}) \| _ {2} ^ {2},\tag{8}
$$

where $\mu _ { \phi , t }$ is the posterior mean inferred by the IDM and sg(·) denotes stop-gradient.

This sequence-level design avoids requiring pointwise alignment between raw commands and latent actions. Such alignment can be unstable because raw commands are mediated by embodimentspecific kinematics, controllers, delays, and contact dynamics. Sec. 4.3 empirically compares this sequence-level interface with pointwise action-to-latent prediction and direct raw-action conditioning.

## 3.5 Training Objective

Given a latent video tensor v, we sample a diffusion timestep $\tau$ and noise ϵ, and form

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {\tau} = (1 - \sigma_ {\tau}) \boldsymbol {v} + \sigma_ {\tau} \boldsymbol {\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol {u} _ {\tau} = \boldsymbol {\epsilon} - \boldsymbol {v}.\tag{9}
$$

The action-conditioned FDM predicts the flow-matching velocity $\hat { \mathbf { u } } _ { \tau } = g _ { \theta } ( \tilde { \pmb { v } } _ { \tau } , \tau , z _ { 1 : T - 1 } )$ , where ${ \pmb u } _ { \tau }$ denotes the diffusion flow target and is distinct from the unified latent action in Sec. 3.2. We train it with $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { r e c } } = \mathbb { E } _ { { \pmb x } _ { 1 : T } , \tau , \epsilon } \left[ \| \hat { { \pmb u } } _ { \tau } - { \pmb u } _ { \tau } \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } \right]$ . The overall objective is

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{total}} = \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{rec}} + \beta \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{KL}} + \lambda_ {\mathrm{adv}} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{GRL}},\tag{10}
$$

where $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { r e c } }$ enforces predictive sufficiency, ${ \mathcal { L } } _ { \mathrm { K I } }$ encourages compactness, and $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { G R I } }$ <sub>L</sub> discourages embodiment-specific shortcuts.

## 4.2 Latent-Interface Transfer Evaluation

We first evaluate whether the learned latent action provides a better conditioning interface for world modeling. For latent-action methods, the IDM infers latent actions from evaluation transitions, and the FDM predicts future dynamics conditioned on these inferred latents. This setting is a representation diagnostic: it measures whether the latent action explains realized transitions and transfers across domains. ## 5 Conclusion and Discussion

SCAR moves beyond the default assumption that a world model must be conditioned on the raw command space of a particular embodiment. Instead, it treats action as a latent factor of visual change: the part of a transition that explains how an agent intervenes in the world, separated from the embodiment-specific command used to realize that intervention. By learning this factor through inverse-forward dynamics, and constraining it to be compact and less embodiment-discriminative, SCAR provides a transferable action interface for pretrained video dynamics models. The gains across Procgen and Robotwin, together with reduced embodiment leakage, suggest that visual transitions can reveal shared action structure without action supervision for latent discovery.

This work is therefore a step toward world models whose action interface is defined by controllable change rather than robot-specific commands. Posterior-latent evaluation isolates the representationlearning problem, while A2L shows that the learned latent space can be connected back to raw commands. Scaling to larger real-robot datasets, in-the-wild videos, and closed-loop policy learning remains an important next step. More broadly, SCAR suggests that transferable world models require not only better visual prediction, but also better abstractions of action itself.

Intuition. The gradient-reversal layer with a cross-entropy objective encourages the learned latent space to be invariant to embodiment identity, yielding a unified latent action space across embodiments. This shared space supports the forward dynamics of the world model, where selfsupervised conditioning enforces semantic structure, promoting both cross-embodiment consistency and alignment with an underlying unified action space.

Proof Sketch. Our result is inspired by Reizinger et al. [43] but differs fundamentally. Prior work shows that minimizing cross-entropy under a vMF distribution recovers the latent space up to a linear transform, relying on alignment with auxiliary variables. In contrast, we maximize cross-entropy with respect to embodiment identity, enforcing invariance rather than alignment. Although their result is not directly applicable, the resulting saddle-point objective still promotes a semantically meaningful latent space. Leveraging this structure and the first-stage representation, we align latent transitions via the inverse dynamics model, matching trajectories in the data-generating process and thereby encouraging latent recovery. Unlike prior work that recovers a variant space, our goal is to recover the invariant structure underlying such variations.

## A.2 Stage 2: embodiment-invariant identifiability up to invertible bijection

Let $f _ { 2 } : \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { a } }  \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ be the second-stage encoder, $z : = f _ { 2 } ( \tilde { \mathbf { \Gamma } } ( \tilde { \mathbf { a } } )$ , and let $D _ { \omega } : \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }  \Delta ^ { | \varepsilon | - 1 }$ be a softmax classifier with rows $\{ { w _ { e } } \} _ { e \in \mathcal { E } }$ producing

$$
D _ {\omega} (e \mid \boldsymbol {z}) = \frac {\exp \langle \boldsymbol {w} _ {e} , \boldsymbol {z} \rangle}{\sum_ {e ^ {\prime} \in \mathcal {E}} \exp \langle \boldsymbol {w} _ {e ^ {\prime}} , \boldsymbol {z} \rangle}.
$$

The encoder–classifier pair is trained against the population cross-entropy

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{CE}} (\omega , f _ {2}) := \mathbb {E} _ {(\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}}, e)} \bigl [ - \log D _ {\omega} (e \mid f _ {2} (\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}})) \bigr ],
$$

where the gradient-reversal layer makes the encoder $f _ { 2 }$ maximize $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { C E } }$ while the classifier ω minimizes it. We study the saddle of this minimax game.

Intuition. The proof is a discriminator–encoder duality argument tailored to the von Mises–Fisher (vMF) structure [44] of a˜. The cross-entropy + vMF + “affine generator” machinery used here originated in the supervised identifiability proofs of Reizinger et al. [43]; the new ingredient is the outer sign reversal on the encoder, which lands the encoder in the orthogonal complement $V ^ { \perp }$ of the cluster-difference subspace rather than aligning with it. We derive everything below from first principles; no result is invoked by reference.

Assumption 2 (vMF on a˜ with partial-rank cluster span). a˜ lies on $\mathbb { S } ^ { d _ { a } - 1 }$ , with embodimentconditional distribution

$$
p (\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} \mid e) \propto \exp \bigl (\kappa \left\langle \boldsymbol {m} _ {e}, \tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} \right\rangle \bigr), \qquad e \in \mathcal {E},\tag{12}
$$

where $\{ m _ { e } \} _ { e \in \mathcal { E } } \subseteq \mathbb { S } ^ { d _ { a } - 1 }$ are unit-norm cluster centers. Define the embodiment-cluster subspace

$$
V := \operatorname{span} \left\{\boldsymbol {m} _ {e} - \boldsymbol {m} _ {e ^ {\prime}}: e, e ^ {\prime} \in \mathcal {E} \right\} \subseteq \mathbb {R} ^ {d _ {a}}, \quad \dim V = d _ {a} - d _ {z},
$$

so dim $V ^ { \perp } = d _ { z }$ . Equivalently, $\{ m _ { e } \}$ is an affine generator system of an affine subspace of $\mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { a } }$ of dimension $d _ { a } - d _ { z }$

Assumption 3 (Linear encoder + non-collapse). The Stage-2 encoder is restricted to linear maps $f _ { 2 } ( { \tilde { \mathbf { a } } } ) { \bar { = } } M { \tilde { \mathbf { a } } }$ with $M \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } \times d _ { a } }$ . The encoder is non-collapsed in the sense that rank $( M ) = d _ { z }$ and, for every $e \in { \mathcal { E } }$ , the composition $M \circ \rho \circ h ( \cdot , e ) : \mathcal { Z } \to \bar { \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } } }$ is injective.

Theorem 2 (Embodiment invariance + unified latent action space recovery and cross-embodiment transfer). Assume Assumption 1, Lemma 1, and Assumptions 2 and 3, with ${ \dot { d } } _ { z } = d _ { a } - \dim V$ . Let $f _ { 2 }$ globally optimize the gradient-reversal cross-entropy objective on e (acting on a˜). Then:

(a) Embodiment invariance. z ⊥⊥ $e ,$ equivalently $p ( z \mid e ) = p ( z ) f o$ r every $e \in { \mathcal { E } } .$

(b) Unified latent action space recovery and cross-embodiment transfer. For every $e \in { \mathcal { E } } _ { : }$ , the composition

$$
\Phi_ {e}: \mathcal {Z} \to \mathbb {R} ^ {d _ {z}}, \quad \Phi_ {e} (\boldsymbol {u}) := f _ {2} \bigl (\rho (h (\boldsymbol {u}, e)) \bigr),
$$

is a continuous bijection. The family $\{ \Phi _ { e } \} _ { e \in \mathcal { E } }$ shares a common pushforward law:

$$
\left(\Phi_ {e}\right) _ {\#} p (\boldsymbol {u}) = p (\boldsymbol {z}) \text {   for   every   } e \in \mathcal {E}.
$$

Because $\Phi _ { e }$ is itself indexed by $e ,$ recovering the unified latent u from the inferred z requires the embodiment $I D e ,$ , and cross-embodiment transfer between $e _ { 1 }$ and $e _ { 2 }$ is realized by the alignment map $\Phi _ { e _ { 2 } } \circ \Phi _ { e _ { 1 } } ^ { - 1 }$

Proof. We prove (a) and (b) in sequence. The proof uses six lemmas, each established from first principles.

## Part (a): embodiment invariance, z ⊥⊥ e.

Step 1 (cross-entropy decomposition). Write $z = f _ { 2 } ( \tilde { \mathbf { a } } )$ . For any encoder $f _ { 2 }$ and classifier $D _ { \omega }$ expand the cross-entropy by inserting log $p ( e \mid z )$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{CE}} (\omega , f _ {2}) & = \mathbb {E} _ {(\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}}, e)} \bigl [ - \log D _ {\omega} (e \mid \boldsymbol {z}) \bigr ] \\ & = \mathbb {E} _ {(\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}}, e)} \bigl [ - \log p (e \mid \boldsymbol {z}) \bigr ] + \mathbb {E} _ {(\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}}, e)} \biggl [ \log \frac {p (e \mid \boldsymbol {z})}{D _ {\omega} (e \mid \boldsymbol {z})} \biggr ] \\ & = H (e \mid \boldsymbol {z}) + \mathbb {E} _ {\boldsymbol {z}} \bigl [ D _ {\mathrm{KL}} \bigl (p (\cdot \mid \boldsymbol {z}) \| D _ {\omega} (\cdot \mid \boldsymbol {z}) \bigr) \bigr ]. \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

The first term, the conditional entropy of e given z, depends only on the joint $p ( e , z )$ , hence only on $f _ { 2 }$ (not ω). The second term is non-negative since each $D _ { \mathrm { K L } }$ is non-negative.

Step 2 (inner classifier optimum). By eq. (13),

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{CE}} (\omega , f _ {2}) \geq H (e \mid \boldsymbol {z}),
$$

with equality if and only if $D _ { \omega } ( \cdot \mid z ) = p ( \cdot \mid z )$ for $p _ { z }$ -almost every z. The inner softmax classifier family is rich enough to realize any conditional distribution on $\mathcal { E }$ given $z \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ (sufficient set of weights $\{ { \pmb w } _ { e } \}$ exists). Hence

$$
\min _ {\omega} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{CE}} (\omega , f _ {2}) = H (e \mid \boldsymbol {z}), \quad \text { attained   at } D _ {\omega} ^ {\star} (\cdot \mid \boldsymbol {z}) = p (\cdot \mid \boldsymbol {z}).\tag{14}
$$

Step 3 (information-theoretic identity). Using $H ( e \mid z ) = H ( e ) - I ( e ; z )$ and that $H ( e )$ is constant in $f _ { 2 }$ (the marginal of e is fixed by the data), eq. (14) becomes

$$
\min _ {\omega} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{CE}} (\omega , f _ {2}) = H (e) - I (e; \boldsymbol {z}).\tag{15}
$$

Step 4 (outer maximization under gradient reversal). The gradient-reversal layer reverses the sign of $\partial \bar { \mathcal { L } } _ { \mathrm { C E } } / \partial \phi$ in the encoder update, while leaving $\partial \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { C E } } / \partial \omega$ unchanged. The classifier therefore still minimizes $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { C E } }$ in $\omega ,$ while the encoder maximizes it in $f _ { 2 }$ . The resulting saddle problem is

$$
\sup _ {f _ {2}} \min _ {\omega} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{CE}} (\omega , f _ {2}) = \sup _ {f _ {2}} \bigl [ H (e) - I (e; \boldsymbol {z}) \bigr ] = H (e) - \inf _ {f _ {2}} I (e; \boldsymbol {z}).\tag{16}
$$

Mutual information is non-negative, $I ( e ; z ) \ge 0$ , with equality if and only $\mathrm { i f } \ z \ \perp \vdash \ e$ . To establish that the infimum in $: _ { f _ { 2 } } I = 0$ is attained, exhibit any feasible encoder achieving it: take $M _ { 0 } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } \times d _ { a } }$ to be a matrix whose rows form an orthonormal basis of $V ^ { \perp }$ (which exists with dim $V ^ { \perp } = d _ { z }$ <sub>z</sub> by Assumption 2). For this $M _ { 0 }$ , the rotational-symmetry computation in Step 5 below shows $z = M _ { 0 } \tilde { { \pmb a } }$ has $I ( e ; z ) = 0$ . Hence

$$
\sup _ {f _ {2}} \min _ {\omega} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{CE}} (\omega , f _ {2}) = H (e).
$$

At the saddle, $I ( e ; z ^ { \star } ) = 0$ , equivalently

$$
z ^ {\star} \perp \perp e.
$$

This proves (a).

## Part (b): unified latent action space recovery and cross-embodiment transfer.

Step 5 (linear pushforward of vMF: distribution depends on e only through ${ \cal M } m _ { e } )$ . Fix the linear encoder $f _ { 2 } ( { \tilde { \pmb a } } ) = M { \tilde { \pmb a } }$ with $\dot { M } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } \times d _ { a } }$ . We compute the moment generating function (MGF) of $z = M \tilde { \mathbf { a } }$ given e.

For any test direction u˜ $\in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ , set $u : = M ^ { \top } \tilde { u } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { a } }$ . Then

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \exp \left(\langle \tilde {u}, M \tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} \rangle\right) \mid e \right] = \mathbb {E} \left[ \exp \left(\langle u, \tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} \rangle\right) \mid e \right].\tag{17}
$$

Plug in the vMF density from eq. (12) with normalizing constant $C ( \kappa ) > 0 \colon$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathbb {E} \bigl [ \exp (\langle u, \tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} \rangle)   \big |   e \bigr ] & = C (\kappa) \int_ {\mathbb {S} ^ {d _ {a} - 1}} \exp (\langle u, x \rangle) \cdot \exp (\kappa \langle \boldsymbol {m} _ {e}, x \rangle)   d \sigma (x) \\ & = C (\kappa) \int_ {\mathbb {S} ^ {d _ {a} - 1}} \exp (\langle u + \kappa \boldsymbol {m} _ {e}, x \rangle)   d \sigma (x), \end{array}\tag{18}
$$

where $\sigma$ is the surface measure on $\mathbb { S } ^ { d _ { a } - 1 }$

The integral on the right depends on $u + \kappa m _ { e }$ only through its norm, by rotational invariance of σ:

$$
\int_ {\mathbb {S} ^ {d _ {a} - 1}} \exp (\langle w, x \rangle) d \sigma (x) = \Psi (\| w \|), \quad \Psi (r) := (2 \pi) ^ {d _ {a} / 2} r ^ {1 - d _ {a} / 2} I _ {d _ {a} / 2 - 1} (r),
$$

with $I _ { \nu }$ the modified Bessel function of the first kind. (For $w \ne 0$ , choose any rotation $R \in S O ( d _ { a } )$ taking $w \mathrm { \bf ~ t o ~ } \| w \| \mathbf { e } _ { 1 } ;$ the integral is invariant under R because dσ is.) Compute $\| u + \kappa m _ { e } \| ^ { 2 }$ using $\| \pmb { m } _ { e } \| = 1$ :

$$
\left\| u + \kappa \boldsymbol {m} _ {e} \right\| ^ {2} = \left\| u \right\| ^ {2} + 2 \kappa \langle u, \boldsymbol {m} _ {e} \rangle + \kappa^ {2}.
$$

The only e-dependence is via the inner product $\langle u , \pmb { m } _ { e } \rangle = \langle M ^ { \top } \tilde { u } , \pmb { m } _ { e } \rangle = \langle \tilde { u } , M \pmb { m } _ { e } \rangle$ . Substituting back into eq. (17):

$$
\mathbb {E} \left[ \exp \left(\langle \tilde {u}, M \tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} \rangle\right) \mid e \right] = C (\kappa) \Psi \left(\sqrt {\| M ^ {\top} \tilde {u} \| ^ {2} + 2 \kappa \langle \tilde {u} , M \boldsymbol {m} _ {e} \rangle + \kappa^ {2}}\right).\tag{19}
$$

The right-hand side is a function of $\left. \tilde { u } , M \pmb { m } _ { e } \right.$ only (the other terms do not depend on e).

Step 6 (invariance forces $M \mathbf { m } _ { e } = M \mathbf { m } _ { e ^ { \prime } } ) .$ . By Part $( \mathbf { a } ) , z \perp \perp e ,$ , so the MGF in eq. (19) is independent of e for every $\tilde { u } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ . Since the right-hand side is a function of $\left. \tilde { u } , M m _ { e } \right.$ only, and Ψ is strictly monotone on $( 0 , \infty )$ (the modified Bessel $I _ { d _ { a } / 2 - 1 } ( r )$ is strictly increasing in $r > 0 )$ , e-independence of the MGF for every u˜ implies

$$
\langle \tilde {u}, M \boldsymbol {m} _ {e} \rangle = \langle \tilde {u}, M \boldsymbol {m} _ {e ^ {\prime}} \rangle \quad \forall \tilde {u} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {d _ {z}}, \forall e, e ^ {\prime} \in \mathcal {E}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\left| M \boldsymbol {m} _ {e} = M \boldsymbol {m} _ {e ^ {\prime}} \forall e, e ^ {\prime} \in \mathcal {E} \right| \iff M (\boldsymbol {m} _ {e} - \boldsymbol {m} _ {e ^ {\prime}}) = 0 \forall e, e ^ {\prime} \Longleftrightarrow \operatorname{row} (M) \subseteq V ^ {\perp},\tag{20}
$$

where the last equivalence uses the definition $V = \mathrm { s p a n } \{ m _ { e } - m _ { e ^ { \prime } } \}$ from Assumption 2, so $V ^ { \perp } = \left\{ v \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { a } } : \left. v , m _ { e } - m _ { e ^ { \prime } } \right. = 0 \forall e , e ^ { \prime } \right\}$

Step 7 (rank + dimension match $\Rightarrow \operatorname { r o w } ( M ) = V ^ { \bot } )$ . By Assumption 3, $\mathrm { r a n k } ( M ) = d _ { z }$ . By Assumption 2, dim $V ^ { \perp } = d _ { a } - \mathrm { d i m } V = d _ { z }$ . Combined with eq. (20), the row space of M is a $d _ { z }$ -dimensional subspace of the $d _ { z }$ -dimensional space $V ^ { \perp }$ , hence

$$
\operatorname{row} (M) = V ^ {\perp}.
$$

Equivalently, ker $M = V$ , and M restricted to $V ^ { \perp }$ is a linear isomorphism $V ^ { \perp } \to \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$

Step 8 (per-embodiment chain is a continuous injection). Fix any $e \in { \mathcal { E } }$ . Define

$$
\Phi_ {e}: \mathcal {Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb {R} ^ {d _ {z}}, \quad \Phi_ {e} (\boldsymbol {u}) := M \bigl (\rho (h (\boldsymbol {u}, e)) \bigr) = f _ {2} (\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}} (\boldsymbol {u}, e)).
$$

$\Phi _ { e }$ is a composition of three continuous maps:

(i) $h ( \cdot , e ) : \mathcal { Z } $ A is continuous and injective in u (by Assumption 1, item (iii)).

(ii) $\rho : { \mathcal { A } } $ A is invertible, hence in particular continuous and injective (by Lemma 1).

(iii) $M : \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { a } }  \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ is linear, hence continuous.

Continuity of the composition is immediate. Injectivity of the composition $\Phi _ { e } = M \circ \rho \circ h ( \cdot , e )$ is exactly the second clause of Assumption 3.

Step 9 (Brouwer’s invariance of domain $\Rightarrow \Phi _ { e }$ is a continuous bijection). The latent space $\mathcal { Z }$ is a $d _ { z }$ -dimensional topological manifold (by Assumption 1, item (i)), and $\Phi _ { e }$ maps into $\dot { \mathbb { R } } ^ { d _ { z } }$ , also of dimension $d _ { z }$ . By Brouwer’s invariance-of-domain theorem, any continuous injection from an open subset of $\mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ into $\mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { z } }$ is an open map and a homeomorphism onto its image. Applied to $\Phi _ { e }$ :

$$
\Phi_ {e}: \mathcal {Z} \to \mathbb {R} ^ {d _ {z}}
$$

is a homeomorphism, in particular a continuous bijection.

Step 10 (common pushforward law). By Assumption 1 item (ii), u ⊥⊥ e, so the conditional distribution $p ( \pmb { u } \mid e ) = p ( \pmb { u } )$ for every $e \in { \mathcal { E } }$ . Hence the conditional distribution of z given e is the pushforward of $p ( \pmb { u } )$ under $\Phi _ { e } \colon$

$$
p (\boldsymbol {z} \mid e) = (\Phi_ {e}) _ {\#} p (\boldsymbol {u}).
$$

By Part (a), z ⊥⊥ e, so $p ( z \mid e ) = p ( z )$ for every e. Combining,

$$
(\Phi_ {e}) _ {\#} p (\boldsymbol {u}) = p (\boldsymbol {z}) \quad \forall e \in \mathcal {E}.
$$

The maps $\Phi _ { e }$ are bijections (Step 9) that all push the same prior $p ( \pmb { u } )$ to the same marginal $p ( z )$ .

## E Implementation Details

Video preprocessing. Episodes are sampled at 20 FPS. Each training sample contains $T = 4 9$ frames, including 17 context frames and 32 future frames. Images are resized to 128×128, normalized to [−1, 1], and encoded by the frozen Wan causal VAE.

Temporal alignment. The Wan causal VAE temporally compresses videos with stride $s _ { \mathrm { v a e } } = 4$ Thus, a $T = { \bar { 4 } } 9 – \mathrm { f r a m e }$ window is mapped to $F = 1 3$ latent timesteps. The IDM operates on the latent-video sequence $v _ { 1 : F } ,$ but produces frame-rate latent action tokens. The temporal groups are

$$
\left\{\boldsymbol {z} _ {1} \right\}, \left\{\boldsymbol {z} _ {2: 5} \right\}, \left\{\boldsymbol {z} _ {6: 9} \right\}, \dots , \left\{\boldsymbol {z} _ {4 6: 4 9} \right\},
$$

where $z _ { 1 }$ corresponds to the first frame and each later 4-token block corresponds to one VAE latent timestep. When comparing to raw actions, we use the transition-aligned part $z _ { 2 : T }$ and re-index it as $z _ { 1 : T - 1 }$ . The action encoder $A _ { \psi }$ maps these frame-rate latent actions back to the VAE latent timeline using a stride-4 causal temporal convolution, producing $c _ { 1 : F }$ for FDM conditioning

Context conditioning and diffusion forcing. The 17 context frames correspond to $F _ { \mathrm { h i s t } } = 5$ VAE latent tokens. During training, we use diffusion forcing [45] in latent space by sampling a latent-time noise schedule $\tau _ { 1 : F }$ . For each latent timestep f ,

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {v}} _ {\tau , f} = (1 - \sigma_ {\tau_ {f}}) \boldsymbol {v} _ {f} + \sigma_ {\tau_ {f}} \boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {f}, \quad \boldsymbol {\epsilon} _ {f} \sim \mathcal {N} (0, I).
$$

History and future tokens may receive different noise levels; with probability $p _ { \mathrm { c l e a n } }$ , history tokens are kept clean by setting their noise level to zero. The FDM predicts the flow target on the full latent sequence,

$$
\hat {\pmb {u}} _ {\tau} = g _ {\theta} \big (\tilde {\pmb {v}} _ {\tau}, \tau_ {1: F}, \pmb {c} _ {1: F} \big), \qquad \pmb {u} _ {\tau} = \pmb {\epsilon} - \pmb {v},
$$

and the reconstruction loss is averaged over latent timesteps. At inference time, the first $F _ { \mathrm { h i s t } }$ latent tokens are clamped to the clean target context latents, and only future tokens are generated.

IDM architecture. The IDM takes latent observation windows as input and outputs a diagonal Gaussian posterior over latent actions. It uses 6 transformer layers, hidden dimension 256, 4 attention heads, and latent action dimension $d _ { z } = 6 4$

FDM and action injection. The FDM is initialized from pretrained Wan weights, while the causal VAE remains frozen. Latent actions are projected by $A _ { \psi }$ , implemented as a lightweight action encoder with a two-layer MLP, causal temporal convolution, and linear modulation head. The resulting action condition is injected through AdaLN modulation at all Wan transformer blocks. We train the Wan diffusion model and the action encoder.

Raw action processing. For Robotwin, embodiment-specific raw actions are padded to a shared 16-dimensional action interface. For Procgen, raw-action baselines use the one-hot discrete action representation. Latent-action methods do not use raw action labels during latent action discovery; raw actions are used only for raw-action baselines and action-to-latent controller experiments.

Optimization. We sample diffusion timesteps τ in continuous time from a uniform distribution and apply the Wan time-shift schedule. Models are trained with AdamW for $1 0 { , } 0 0 0$ steps. ## F Action-Transfer Embodiment Leakage Evaluation

We provide additional details for the embodiment-leakage evaluation used in Table 2. The goal of this evaluation is to quantify whether cross-embodiment action transfer preserves the target robot embodiment or leaks source-embodiment appearance.

Protocol. We use Franka as the target embodiment and transfer latent actions from three source embodiments:

$$
\{\text { aloha - agilex,   arx - x5,   ur5 } \}.
$$

For each source embodiment, we sample 50 source-target pairs. Source trajectories are drawn from the source training split, while target visual contexts are drawn from the Franka evaluation split. For each pair, the IDM extracts a latent action sequence from the source trajectory, and the FDM generates a transferred rollout conditioned on the Franka context.

The generated video contains both target-context frames and predicted future frames. Since the context frames are copied from the target embodiment, including them would artificially inflate target-embodiment scores. We therefore evaluate only the predicted future segment. In our setting, the context length is 17 frames and the predicted future horizon is 32 frames.

Embodiment classifier. We train an independent single-frame embodiment classifier on groundtruth Robotwin frames from place\_a2b\_left. The classifier predicts one of four embodiment labels:

$$
\{\text { aloha,   arx,   franka,   ur5 } \}.
$$

The classifier is a lightweight CNN with three convolutional blocks. Each block uses a $3 \times 3$ convolution followed by ReLU activation, with channel widths 32, 64, and 128. The first two blocks are followed by $2 \times 2$ max pooling, and the final feature map is reduced by global average pooling before a linear classifier. The model is trained on the training episodes of each embodiment and evaluated on held-out validation episodes. It achieves 97.1% validation top-1 accuracy. During leakage evaluation, the classifier is frozen and applied frame-by-frame to transferred videos.

Metrics. Let $p _ { e } ( f )$ denote the classifier probability of embodiment e on predicted frame $f .$ For a source embodiment $e _ { s }$ and target embodiment $e _ { t } ,$ , we compute

$$
\text { SourceProb } = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {F} |} \sum_ {f \in \mathcal {F}} p _ {e _ {s}} (f),\tag{21}
$$

$$
\text { TargetProb } = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {F} |} \sum_ {f \in \mathcal {F}} p _ {e _ {t}} (f),\tag{22}
$$

where $\mathcal { F }$ denotes the predicted future frames. We also report a two-way target share,

$$
\text { TargetShare } = \frac {\text { TargetProb }}{\text { TargetProb } + \text { SourceProb }},\tag{23}
$$

and the target-source margin,

$$
\mathrm{TargetSource} = \mathrm{TargetProb} - \mathrm{SourceProb}.\tag{24}
$$

Finally, we report the analogous top-1 target-source margin by comparing the fraction of predicted frames classified as the target embodiment against the fraction classified as the source embodiment.

All reported leakage metrics are averaged over the three source embodiments.

## H Broader Impact, Limitations, and Future Work

Potential positive impacts. SCAR studies self-supervised continuous action representation learning for embodied world models. Its main contribution is a transferable latent action interface that reduces reliance on embodiment-specific raw command representations. If developed responsibly, such representations may improve data efficiency, support transfer across robot platforms, and enable more simulation-based testing before real-world deployment. This may benefit applications such as robot manipulation, laboratory automation, assistive robotics, and operation in remote or hazardous environments.

Potential negative impacts. The main risk is over-interpreting simulation-based prediction results as evidence of safe real-world control. SCAR is evaluated on world-model prediction and transfer diagnostics, not closed-loop deployment. If the learned latent interface were used in downstream robotic systems without additional validation, model errors or simulation-to-real mismatches could lead to unreliable behavior.

Limitations and future work. Our experiments are conducted on controlled simulation/control benchmarks and focus on world-model prediction rather than closed-loop planning or real-robot execution. This setting helps isolate cross-embodiment action representation learning, but it does not fully capture the diversity, noise, and safety constraints of real-world robot deployment. Future work should scale SCAR to larger and more diverse real-robot datasets, study closed-loop planning and policy learning with the learned latent action interface.

Mitigation and scope. We do not claim that SCAR is ready for safety-critical deployment. Any downstream use should require task-specific safety constraints, real-world validation, monitoring under distribution shift, and human oversight. Released code and models should be accompanied by documentation of intended use, limitations, dataset scope, and evaluation conditions.
