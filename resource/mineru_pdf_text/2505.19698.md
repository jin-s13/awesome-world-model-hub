## Abstract

Recently, Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) have achieved average superhuman level performance on the Atari100k benchmark. However, we discover that conventional aggregates mask a major problem, Performance Asymmetry: MBRL agents dramatically outperform humans in certain tasks (Agent-Optimal tasks) while drastically underperform humans in other tasks (Human-Optimal tasks). Indeed, despite achieving SOTA in the overall mean Human-Normalized Scores (HNS), the SOTA agent scored the worst among baselines on Human-Optimal tasks, with a striking 21× performance gap between the Human/Agent-Optimal subsets. To address this, we partition Atari100k evenly into Human/Agent-Optimal subsets, and introduce a more balanced aggregate, Sym-HNS. We further trace the striking Performance Asymmetry in the SOTA pixel diffusion world model to the curse of dimensionality and its prowess on high visual detail tasks (e.g. Breakout). To this end, we propose a novel latent end-to-end Joint Embedding Diffusion (JEDI) world model that achieves SOTA results in Sym-HNS, Human-Optimal tasks, and Breakout—thus reversing the worsening Performance Asymmetry trend while improving computational efficiency and remaining competitive on the full Atari100k.

## 1. Introduction

Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) centers on learning a model of the agent's environment dynamics known as a World Model (Sutton, 1991; Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018). The principle accompanying MBRL is rooted in broader theories of intelligence; (Ma et al., 2022) asserts parsimony and self-consistency as foundational for the emergence of intelligence—a view shared by LeCun (2022) and echoes earlier works by Friston et al. (2006). In the MBRL paradigm introduced by Hafner et al. (2020a), parsimony is achieved by compressing high-dimensional images to a low-dimensional latent space using the encoder, and it is partly learned through the self-consistency objective of aligning the trajectories generated by its dynamics model with actual observations.

This paradigm is effective, recently crossing the mean human-level performance (Micheli et al., 2022; Robine et al., 2023; Hafner et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023) on the challenging Atari100k benchmark (Kaiser et al., 2019). Since Atari100k limits the agent to 100K environment steps or around two hours of human gameplay, this is a seminal milestone for RL as one of its core limitations was poor data efficiency. However, we discover that crossing mean human-level performance with MBRL on Atari100k can be problematic.

In particular, we uncover a surprising Performance Asymmetry in MBRL performance across the 26 tasks in Atari100k. While the mean Human Normalized Score (HNS) $^{1}$ of more than 1.0 suggests super-human performance, a closer look reveals that MBRL agents massively outperform in tasks with a low human score—what we call Agent-Optimal tasks—and perform overwhelmingly poor in tasks with a high human score—Human-Optimal tasks $^{2}$

This is clearly illustrated in Figure 1: MBRL algorithms generally underperform on Human-Optimal tasks by an order of magnitude compared to Agent-Optimal tasks. Worse still, a worsening trend can be observed with pixel-based agents, with the latest pixel-based method (Alonso et al., 2024) having the most Performance Asymmetry. Indeed, despite achieving an overall state-of-the-art (SOTA) mean HNS of 1.46, the SOTA agent performed the worst among all baselines on Human-Optimal tasks, with a striking $21\times$ performance gap between Human/Agent-Optimal subsets. This raises a critical question: are MBRL agents truly progressing or are they overfitting to certain tasks?

To our knowledge, this work is the first to illuminate Performance Asymmetry in MBRL agents, highlighting the issue of task overfitting. More importantly, this brings attention to the overlooked fact that MBRL is critically failing on certain tasks while performing exceedingly well in others (Figure 2). The analysis of these success and failure modes empowers future research that would have otherwise been overlooked and unrecognized.

We first lay the groundwork by identifying the core issue of current aggregates: (i) the arithmetic mean and (ii) the large value range of HNS. The arithmetic mean is invariant to the extreme values, and the large value range renders meaningful changes in low values overshadowed by small delta in large values. This results in the invisibility of Performance Asymmetry in many conventional aggregates, not only in the average score (Section 2.1). To address this, we first expose Performance Asymmetry by evenly partitioning Atari100k into Agent/Human-Optimal subsets (Section 2). We then introduce Sym-HNS, a more balanced aggregate that overcomes these limitations by leveraging the harmonic mean (Section 2.2).

As the initial foray into tackling this problem, we focus on reversing the worsening trend of Performance Asymmetry over time, specifically the striking asymmetry exhibited by the latest SOTA agent (Figure 1). We trace the underperformance of pixel-based agents in Human-Optimal tasks to the curse of dimensionality from using image inputs to its RL networks (Watter et al., 2015; Oh et al., 2017; Gregor et al., 2019), coupled with the fact that Human-Optimal tasks are generally more complex (Figure 4). In parallel, the SOTA agents' outperformance in Agent-Optimal tasks can be attributed to their outsized performance in tasks with high visual detail (e.g. Breakout), due to the use of a pixel diffusion world model (Alonso et al., 2024). As such, we seek to answer the research questions:

1. Can the striking Performance Asymmetry of a pixelbased agent be mitigated with a latent space world model?

2. Can the use of diffusion in a latent dynamics model overcome the underperformance of latent-based agents on high visual detail tasks?

To this end, we propose the Joint Embedding Diffusion (JEDI) world model, a latent end-to-end diffusion model with its encoder trained using a self-consistent denoising objective inspired by Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) (Assran et al., 2023). To our knowledge, JEDI is the first method that enables the encoder to learn a latent representation that works well for the downstream RL task directly from the gradients of a latent diffusion model's denoising training loss using a JEPA-style framework—without using reconstruction, hidden state extraction, and distillation. This is also the first work that shows that an off-the-shelf diffusion denoising loss (Karras et al., 2022) is directly compatible with JEPA, challenging prevailing beliefs that JEPA requires bespoke loss functions (Grill et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021; Bardes et al., 2024). JEDI yields SOTA performance on Sym-HNS, Human-Optimal tasks and Breakout—thus reversing the worsening Performance Asymmetry trend in MBRL while improving computational efficiency (Figure 12) and remaining competitive on Agent-Optimal tasks and the full Atari100k.

In summary, our contributions are as follows (Table 2).

1. We uncover Performance Asymmetry in MBRL agents, highlighting the problem of task overfitting (Section 2).

2. We lay the groundwork for tackling Performance Asymmetry by partitioning Atari100k into two subsets and introducing a more balanced aggregate, Sym-HNS (Section 2.2).

3. We analyze the striking Performance Asymmetry in pixel-based agents and propose relevant research ques-

tions (Section 2.3).

4. We introduce JEDI, a novel latent end-to-end diffusion-based world model to answer the research questions (Section 3) and validate our framework empirically and qualitatively (Section 4, Section 4.2).

Pre-existing methods that achieved a overall mean HNS of around 1.0 did so with super-human performance in only 8 – 11 out of the 26 Atari100k tasks (Table 10). Since HNS is $\frac{\text{Agent Score} - \text{Random Score}}{\text{Human Score} - \text{Random Score}}$ , this implies that exceptional performance in a minority of tasks with low human scores is overcompensating for sub-human performances in majority of tasks. Moreover, none of the baselines outperform the human score in 12 out of 13 Human-Optimal tasks. Indeed, Figure 2 reveals a drastic skew across task performance.

In order to study Performance Asymmetry, we take inspiration from fairness research to identify a Protected Attribute (Barocas et al., 2023) for every task so we can observe the problem clearly. To achieve this, we averaged the mean HNS of four pre-existing baselines that were among the first to approach or surpass the mean HNS of 1 on Atari100k: IRIS (Micheli et al., 2022), TWM (Robine et al., 2023), DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023), and STORM (Zhang et al., 2023). Concretely, we define tasks on which the averaged agent's mean HNS exceeds $75\%$ as Agent-Optimal and those falling below as Human-Optimal. This classification provides a balanced set of 13 tasks per subset (Table 1).

## 2.1. Existing Evaluation Metrics

The core issue with existing metrics is due to (i) the arithmetic mean being invariant to extreme values and (ii) the large value range of HNS $^{3}$ resulting, in large values dominating over low values. As a motivating example, with arbitrary metrics on a benchmark with two tasks comparing methods A and B :

(i) A: (1, 10), B: (5.5, 5.5); Arithmetic mean is equal, masking the extreme scores in A.

(ii) A: (0.1, 11), B: (0.2, 10); Arithmetic mean of A (5.55) outperforms B (5.05). However, B outperforms A in the first task by 100% and yet, A only outperforms B on the second task by 10%.

This issue is prevalent in current arithmetic mean aggregates, such as stratified bootstrap confidence intervals, Inter Quartile Mean (IQM), and the optimality gap proposed by Agarwal et al. (2021). This can be seen from Figure 8c where DIAMOND (Alonso et al., 2024) achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) for almost all metrics with reasonable error bars, yet the Performance Asymmetry in Figure 1 and 2 is not visible at all. Moreover, there are other issues: IQM eliminates entire tasks rather than statistical anomalies, and the optimality gap is blind to progress beyond HNS of 1.0 (Section B).

Agarwal et al. (2021) also proposed performance profiles to address the disparity in task performance. However, the typical plot and large range of HNS draw focus towards the high HNS regime, concealing the low HNS regime (Figure 14).

## 2.2. Sym-HNS

We strive for an aggregate that celebrates progress on all tasks in a balanced manner, exposing extreme data spread while preventing the delta of large values from dominating.

We introduce:

$$
\mathrm{Sym-HNS} = \frac {2 \mu_ {\tau} (\mathrm{AO}) \cdot \mu_ {\tau} (\mathrm{HO})}{\mu_ {\tau} (\mathrm{AO}) + \mu_ {\tau} (\mathrm{HO})}\tag{1}
$$

where $\mu_{\tau}(\mathrm{AO}/\mathrm{HO})$ refers to the mean HNS on the Agent/Human-Optimal subset respectively. Sym-HNS leverages the harmonic mean, as inspired by the F1-Score (Van Rijsbergen, 1979), effectively balancing two equally important metrics by discouraging extreme and dominant values (Figure 3). ## 2.4. Curse of Dimensionality: Underperformance of Pixel-Based Methods on Human-Optimal Tasks

Our first insight on algorithms hinges on the stark finding that pixel-based agents perform much worse in Human-Optimal tasks compared to latent-based agents (Figure 1). This suggests that latent world models can help with Human-Optimal performance.

Action Space Complexity. We hypothesize that the pixel-based agents perform worse in Human-Optimal tasks due to poorer training efficiency (Watter et al., 2015; Oh et al., 2017; Gregor et al., 2019; Hafner et al., 2020a). This is because their RL network input is in $R^{64\times64\times3}$ . The exponential increase in search space due to higher action space disproportionately affects pixel-based agents compared to latent-based agents. Moreover, given the same number of environment steps, pixel-based RL networks need to perform both representation learning and RL training, whereas the RL networks of latent-based agents only have to perform RL training. This is supported empirically in Figure 5 where SOTA pixel-based agent performs significantly worse at tasks with high action space, contributing to its underperformance in Human-Optimal tasks.

## 2.5. Diffusion for Better Agent-Optimal Performance

We observe that out of the two pixel-based agents, DIAMOND significantly outperforms IRIS in Agent-Optimal tasks (Figure 1). This suggests that using a diffusion dynamics model can help with Agent-Optimal performance, as it is the main difference between the two methods. In the paper, Alonso et al. (2024) credited their outperformance over IRIS to the diffusion models' high fidelity generation, enabling better performance in visual tasks, most notably Breakout, an Agent-Optimal task. Indeed, DIAMOND overwhelmingly outperforms in Breakout, with a HNS of 4.54 versus the averaged agents' HNS of 1.25 (Table 1), contributing significantly to its outperformance on Agent-Optimal tasks.

## 3. JEDI: Joint Embedding Diffusion World Model World Model

To answer the above research questions, we introduce the Joint Embedding DIffusion World Model (JEDI), which learns an end-to-end latent space encoder while leveraging a diffusion dynamics model (Figure 6). Mathematically:

$$
\text { World   Model } \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \text { Encoder: } \\ z _ {t} ^ {0} = \mathbf {E} _ {\phi} (x _ {t}), \\ \text { Latent   Diffusion   Dynamics   Model: } \\ \hat {z} _ {t + 1} ^ {0} \sim \mathbf {S} (\mathbf {D} _ {\theta} (\hat {z} _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau}, Z _ {t} ^ {\tau})), \\ \text { Reward   \&   Termination } \\ (\hat {r} _ {t}, \hat {d} _ {t}) = \mathbf {R} _ {\psi} (z _ {t} ^ {0}), \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

where t is the environment time step, $x_{t}$ is the environment observation, $E_{\phi}$ is the encoder, $z_{t}^{0}$ refers to the clean latent at environment time step t, $x_{t} \in [0,1]^{64 \times 64 \times 3}$ is the environment image input and $z_{t} \in [-3,3]^{16 \times 8 \times 8}$ is the latent state, $\tau$ is the diffusion time step, drawn from the Log-Normal (LN) distribution, determining the noise schedule $\sigma(\tau)$ with 0 being clean latent state, $D_{\theta}$ is the single-step conditioned denoising operator and S is the ODE or SDE solver for multi-step denoising inference. $r_{t} \in \{-1,0,1\}$ is the reward and $d_{t} \in \{0,1\}$ is the episode termination flag. We follow the pre-conditioning diffusion paradigm as introduced in (Karras et al., 2022):

$$
\mathbf {D} _ {\theta} (z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau}, Z _ {t} ^ {\tau}) = c _ {\mathrm{skip}} ^ {\tau} z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau} + c _ {\mathrm{out}} ^ {\tau} \mathbf {F} _ {\theta} \big (c _ {\mathrm{in}} ^ {\tau} z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau}, Z _ {t} ^ {\tau} \big)\tag{3}
$$

where, $c_{noise}^{\tau}$ is a fixed transformation of $\tau$ , $Z_{t}^{\tau} := (c_{noise}^{\tau}, z_{t-3:t}^{0}, a_{t-3:t})$ , and $c_{skip}^{\tau}, c_{out}^{\tau}, c_{in}^{\tau}$ are preconditioning variables depending on sampled noise for better behavior of $F_{\theta}$ , the neural network parameterized by $\theta$ .

Joint Embedding Diffusion loss. The loss function of the latent diffusion dynamics model, $\mathcal{L}_{dyn}(\theta,\phi)$ is reparameterized to:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \mathbb {E} _ {z _ {1: T} \sim q, x _ {1: T} \sim p, \tau \sim \mathrm{LN}} \left[ \left\| \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathbf {F} _ {\theta} \big (c _ {\mathrm{in}} ^ {\tau} \mathrm{sg} (z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau}), Z _ {t} ^ {\tau} \big) \right. \right. \\ - \frac {1}{c _ {\mathrm{out}} ^ {\tau}} \big (\mathrm{sg} (z _ {t + 1} ^ {0}) - c _ {\mathrm{skip}} ^ {\tau} \mathrm{sg} (z _ {t + 1} ^ {\tau}) \big) \left\| ^ {2} \right] \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where $q(z_{t}) = (\mathbf{E}_{\phi})_{\#} p(x_{t})$ is the deterministic push forward mapping of $E_{\phi}$ on the randomly sampled $x_{t}$ and sg refers to stop-grad.

During JEDI training (Figure 6a), the encoder $E_{\phi}$ first derives $z_{t-3:t+1}^{0}$ from $x_{t-3:t+1}$ . $z_{t+1}^{\tau}$ is derived by adding $z_{t+1}^{0}$ with stop-grad to the sampled noise which is scaled according to $\tau$ . It is then passed into $F_{\theta}$ together with $Z_{t}^{\tau}$ and it regresses towards the direction of the next latent state using Equation (4). During inference (Figure 6b), the conditioning is derived in the same manner, except $z_{t+1}^{\tau}$ is simply random noise to start the iterative denoising to arrive at the clean latent state (Algorithm 1).

The Cross-Entropy (CE) loss for the Reward and Termination model, $\mathcal{L}_{r\&d}(\psi,\phi)$ , is:

$$
\mathbb {E} _ {z _ {1: T} ^ {0} \sim q, (x _ {1: T}, r _ {1: T}, d _ {1: T}) \sim p} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \mathrm{CE} (\textbf {R} _ {\psi} (z _ {t} ^ {0}), (r _ {t}, d _ {t}))\tag{5}
$$

Preventing Representation Collapse. The encoder learns from both 4 and 5 where the gradients flow back to $\mathbf{E}_{\phi}$ through $z_{t}^{0}$ . In order to prevent representation collapse, stopgrad is applied to $z_{t + 1}^{0}$ and the learning rate of $\mathbf{E}_{\phi}$ is 0.3 that of $\mathbf{F}_{\theta}$ , as inspired from JEPA and TDMPC2 (Assran et al., 2023; Hansen et al., 2023). Crucially, this enables $\mathbf{E}_{\phi}$ to learn directly from $\mathbf{F}_{\theta}$ 's denoising loss gradients without any reconstruction loss.

Stabilising Diffusion in Latent Space. Conventional image diffusion has a natural clamp during denoising inference as $x_{t} \in [0,1]$ . To ensure that the latent denoising process is stable, we impose a differentiable clamp function, $C(z_{t}^{0}) = \tanh(z_{t}^{0}/s)s$ , scaled by a factor, s = 3, on the outputs of $E_{\phi}$ as well as on $D_{\theta}$ . With every batch of trajectory sampled, we randomly switch with uniform probability between using the output of $D_{\theta}$ and $E_{\phi}$ for the subsequent time step's JEDI loss. This is done to balance the capturing of near-horizon information while providing sufficient input data to the encoder.

Policy. The policy is trained in an actor critic framework (Hafner et al., 2020b; Alonso et al., 2024) and the intentional use of REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) ensures that JEDI does not incur expensive gradient computation through the multi-step denoising of the diffusion dynamics model.

## 4.2. Qualitative Analysis

We support our proposed analysis linking curse of dimensionality and task complexity with a qualitative study on BankHeist, DemonAttack, and Hero (Figure 11). In BankHeist, JEDI learns to repeatedly enter and exit maps, gaining easy points from banks that spawn near the exit. We observe similar behaviors in Hero, where the DIAMOND agent fails to destroy obstacles and self-destructs more frequently. In DemonAttack, DIAMOND learns an ineffective policy of staying at one corner and jittering while randomly using FIRE at mostly nothing. JEDI chases enemies and neutralizes them effectively, achieving SOTA super-human performance.

In all cases, DIAMOND struggles to reason about and deploy the FIRE action appropriately. This can be attributed to the use of pixel inputs for its RL networks, which makes learning exponentially harder, especially in complex environments with high action space and complex Shooter dynamics. By moving the diffusion dynamics model into latent space, JEDI is better able to use the FIRE action in context, leading to drastically improved policies.

## 5. Conclusion

We uncover the problem of Performance Asymmetry in MBRL, highlighting how conventional aggregates conceal task overfitting. We reveal this asymmetry by identifying a protected attribute—Agent/Human-Optimality—and propose a more balanced aggregate, Sym-HNS, that addresses the dominance of extreme values. An analysis of the two subsets exposes two distinct quantitative differences: action space and the prevalence of Shooter tasks. We trace the striking Performance Asymmetry in the SOTA agent to the curse of dimensionality associated with image inputs and the use of diffusion, which benefits visually demanding tasks. To this end, we propose JEDI, which leverages an end-to-end latent diffusion dynamics model, that reverses the Performance Asymmetry in the SOTA agent and demonstrates that diffusion in latent-based agents can aid performance on visually bottlenecked tasks (Table 2).

Beyond addressing our motivating questions, our study opens several promising research directions. The roughly $10\times$ gap between Agent/Human-Optimal tasks suggests a compelling avenue for deeper investigation: (i) understanding the fundamental characteristics of tasks in which humans excel versus those in which agents excel, and (ii) the mechanisms within algorithms that drive success or failure on each subset. Finally, our findings motivate the exploration of Performance Asymmetry beyond Atari100k and MBRL more broadly.

## 6. Related Works

MBRL. Early world modelling can be attributed to Dyna (Sutton, 1991). (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018) proposed learning the world model in the latent space from pixels. (Hafner et al., 2019) proposed learning a world model for planning. Dreamer (Hafner et al., 2020a) learns an actorcritic network using the representations learned by an end-to-end trained world model. DreamerV2 (Hafner et al., 2020b) introduced discrete latents. DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023) offers improvements for domain generalization. In parallel, IRIS (Micheli et al., 2022) developed a language model-inspired world model. Multiple works proposed the use of transformer: STORM (Zhang et al., 2023), and TWM (Robine et al., 2023). Chen et al. (2023; 2022) leveraged world models for modeling human belief states. Hansen et al. (2022; 2023) introduced the use of JEPA for learning world model dynamics. Alonso et al. (2024) introduced diffusion as world models for MBRL. JEDI is the first end-to-end latent diffusion world model.

JEPA. Grill et al. (2020) originally proposed the use of a symmetric $L_{2}$ loss and hypothesized that it allows representation collapse to be an unstable solution. Caron et al. (2021) proposed cross-entropy loss and Assran et al. (2023) used $L_{2}$ loss. Bardes et al. (2024); Assran et al. (2025) proposed the use of $L_{1}$ loss, connecting this to the minimization of deviation from the Mean Absolute Median. Our work shows that JEPA training paradigm is directly compatible with off-the-shelf diffusion loss.

Diffusion models was first introduced by Song & Ermon (2019); Ho et al. (2020). Karras et al. (2022) optimized the training framework. Vahdat et al. (2021) proposed latent diffusion trained jointly with reconstruction. Others leverage on pretraining (Rombach et al., 2022; Parker-Holder et al., 2024). It is effective for planning and generating data (Janner et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; Jackson et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2024). Chi et al. (2025) uses diffusion models for joint policy and encoder learning. Diffusion models for representation learning involve searching through denoising outputs (Luo et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2024), leveraging distillation (Yang & Wang, 2023; Zhang et al., 2022), pixel generation conditioned on compressed representation (Pandey et al., 2022; Nielsen et al., 2023), or using the network's hidden activations (Baranchuk et al., 2021; Xiang et al., 2023). Our work presents a novel latent diffusion model that jointly learns and predicts compressed representations using JEPA, effectively used for downstream RL task.

## B. Discussion on Other Aggregates

## B.1. Further Discussion on Inter-Quartile Mean (IQM) and Optimality Gap

Given the reasonable confidence intervals shown in Figure 8c, and the drastic differences in score magnitudes across tasks in Figure 2, it is straightforward to infer that the IQM across all runs will eliminate entire task performances as a whole rather than targeting random seed anomalies across all tasks. This is explicitly shown in Table 5 and Table 6 where we see almost all 5 runs of the same tasks consistently being in the top 25 percentile (e.g. Boxing, CrazyClimber, Krull) and the bottom 25 percentile (e.g. PrivateEye, Seaquest, Freeway) This encourages the forgoing of performance improvements in tasks that lies in these two ranges, especially the bottom 25 percentile.

Optimality Gap averages the mean gap of tasks to score HNS of 1. Unfortunately, it does not take into account the success of agents beyond HNS of 1.0.

$$
\text { Optimality   Gap } = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N} \max (1 - \min (1, \max (0, H N S _ {n})) , 0).
$$

Both aggregates are not aligned with our intent of celebrating performance and all forms of progress across all tasks. Nonetheless, we still report these aggregates in our results.

## B.2. Median

The use of Median in Atari was already criticized in Agarwal et al. (2021) as zero scores on nearly half the tasks do not affect it. This is antithetical to our work on alleviating Performance Asymmetry.

## B.3. Variance/Standard Deviation

One natural aggregate to consider is using the variance/standard deviation of data to measure Performance Asymmetry. However, variance itself is derived using the arithmetic mean. This implies that it is subject to the same issues of extreme and dominant values. For instance, a few tasks having extremely high variance will negate the effects of many tasks being close to the mean. Indeed, from Table 7, all of the standard deviations are relatively high and proportionate to their means. As such we are unable to decisively observe Performance Asymmetry seen in Figure 1 and Figure 2. This is because all of the MBRL agents perform exceptionally well on the same set of tasks with low human scores, resulting in high standard deviation anomalies that mask meaningful gains in other tasks that reduce standard deviation.

## C. Supplementary Background

## C.1. Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

MBRL attempts to solve Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDP) environments (Kaelbling et al., 1998) with RL where the agent does not have direct access to states, $z_{t}$ , and only have access to observations containing partial information $o_{t}$ . The objective is to maximize the expected cumulative sum of discounted rewards. This is typically done through learning a generative world model which consists of an encoder, dynamics model, reward and termination model with SSL. The RL agent is then learned within compressed latent trajectories of the world model, and the trained agent is deployed in the real environment to collect data for training the world model, and the cycle repeats.

## C.2. JEPA

JEPA learns by training a predictor to map target embeddings of equivariant views of data modality in the same latent space. To avoid representation collapse—a problem that occurs when a representation learner tends to represent data with similar embeddings, modern day JEPA generally relies on data augmentation and self teacher-student training (Caron et al., 2021), which is a result of a number of works.

## C.3. Score-based diffusion models and flow matching

Modern diffusion models can be generalized to a continuous score-based generative SDE of a random variable $\{z^{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,T]}$ where $\tau\in[0,T]$ is the diffusion time variable. We are interested in modeling the stochastic process between data and noise, and thus the marginal distributions of z, $\{p^{\tau}\}_{\tau\in[0,T]}$ , begins with the data distribution $p^{0}$ and ends with a Gaussian, $p^{T}$ . The forward process from data to noise is modeled as a diffusion process, and it turns out that the reverse process, from noise to data is also a diffusion process, given by the following SDEs: a formalization of adding cumulative infinitesimal Gaussian noise over time learning and optimal score model appears to involve discretising and solving of the forward and backward SDE Modern diffusion models can be generalized to a continuous score-based generative SDE which flows forward and backward from gaussian noise to data (Anderson, 1982; Song et al., 2020):

$$
d \mathbf {z} = \mathbf {f} (\mathbf {z}, \tau) d \tau + g (\tau) d \mathbf {w}\tag{6}
$$

$$
d \mathbf {z} = [ \mathbf {f} (\mathbf {z}, \tau) - g (\tau) ^ {2} \nabla_ {\mathbf {z}} \log p ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {z}) ] d \tau + g (\tau) d \bar {\mathbf {w}}\tag{7}
$$

where $\mathbf{w}$ is the Wiener process, and $g(\tau):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is the amplitude of noise at time $\tau$ . $\mathbf{f}(.,\tau):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the drift coefficient (typically 0). $\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log p^{\tau}(\mathbf{z})$ is the score function that determines the direction from noise to data given $\tau$ , which can be estimated with a neural network score model $\mathbf{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\tau)$ . Modern diffusion models can be generalized to a continuous score-based generative SDE which flows forward and backward from gaussian noise to data(Anderson, 1982; Song et al., 2020). At first glance, learning a neural network $F_{\theta}$ for this resembles earlier works that require solving an ODE during training(Chen et al., 2018; Rezende & Mohamed, 2015). However, the main novelty of diffusion models lies in the fact that simulation-free efficient and unbiased training can be achieved because a specific sample-conditional path was pre-determined. Surprisingly, the intractable objective of regressing $\mathbf{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\tau)$ towards the marginal probability $\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{z})$ at diffusion time step $\tau$ :

$$
\mathcal {L} (\theta) = \mathbb {E} _ {\tau , z ^ {0}} [ | | \mathbf {F} _ {\theta} (\mathbf {z} ^ {\tau}, \tau) - \nabla_ {\mathbf {z} ^ {\tau}} \log \mathbf {p} ^ {\tau} (\mathbf {z}) | | ^ {2} ]\tag{8}
$$

has the same gradient as regressing towards the tractable sample-conditional vector field derived from our choice of probability path in expectation (Albergo et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2022):

$$
\mathcal {L} (\theta) = \mathbb {E} _ {\tau , z ^ {0}} [ | | \mathbf {F} _ {\theta} (\mathbf {z} ^ {\tau}, \tau) - \nabla_ {\mathbf {z} ^ {\tau}} \log \mathbf {p} ^ {\mathbf {0} \tau} (\mathbf {z} ^ {\tau} | \mathbf {z} ^ {\mathbf {0}}) | | ^ {2} ]\tag{9}
$$

where the expectation is over $\tau$ , and the noised sample $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathbf{p}^{\mathbf{0}\tau}(\mathbf{z}^{\tau}|\mathbf{z}^{\mathbf{0}})$ , depends on the pre-determined probability path conditional on clean samples $z^{0} \sim p^{0}$ . This can be derived from linear interpolation or the standard diffusion path at time step $\tau$ . With this, the second term for Eqn 9 becomes trivial.

## D. Changes Compared to Baseline and Hyperparameters

We used the DIAMOND library (Alonso et al., 2024) (MIT License) for our implementation. The main difference between JEDI World Model and DIAMOND is the transplant of the DIAMOND's RL encoder onto the JEDI World Model and the techniques implemented to facilitate end-to-end latent diffusion mentioned in Section 3. The only network architectural changes are practical: (1) changing the original RL encoder to downsample to $z_{t} \in [-3,3]^{16 \times 8 \times 8}$ , (2) modifying the diffusion UNET (Ronneberger et al., 2015) into a single layer without downsampling, (3) and removing the downsampling in the Reward/Termination Model's encoder. The only hyperparameter changes were increasing the diffusion model's warm-up learning steps to 1000 and sigma of input data to be 1.
